# A Priori Logical Abilities (`C-02`)

## Formulation

`A Priori Logical Abilities` (`APLA`) is the pre-theoretic epistemic competence by which a rational thinker can recognize logical properties and relations—such as deductive validity, invalidity, and inconsistency—without conducting empirical investigation into the external world.

## Explanation

When you reason from "All whales are mammals" and "Moby is a whale" to "Moby is a mammal," it seems you can tell that the inference is logically valid just by thinking, without running any scientific experiments. Philosophers fight over this ability because if thought meanings depend on the external world ([[Content Externalism]]), an unnoticed change of environment could make two uses of the same word in a single argument secretly refer to different things—turning a seemingly valid deduction into a fallacy of equivocation that you cannot detect from the armchair. To decide whether externalism destroys basic logical competence, theorists dispute what our armchair logical ability actually covers: concrete thoughts with world-dependent meanings, abstract logical templates, or the internal mental symbols we manipulate.

### Candidate Sharpenings
- [[Token-Level Wide APLA Definition]] (`D-09`) — The ability to tell purely by reflection whether a specific, concrete train of thought is valid or invalid, taking into account its full world-dependent meanings.
- [[Form-Level APLA Definition]] (`D-10`) — The ability to recognize abstract logical patterns (such as "if A then B; A; therefore B") as valid purely by reflection, without knowing whether two concrete thought occurrences refer to the same thing.
- [[Narrow-Syntactic APLA Definition]] (`D-11`) — Armchair sensitivity to the internal mental structure or symbol-matching of one's thoughts, which explains why a thinker is rationally ready to draw an inference even when external meanings shift.

## Usage in Literature

[[Paul Boghossian]] introduces `A Priori Logical Abilities` in the demanding token-level wide sense (`D-09`), arguing that if a thinker cannot tell a priori whether a concrete deduction like Peter's *Pavarotti* inference is valid or commits a fallacy of equivocation, then content externalism destroys our most elementary logical capacities ([[Boghossian 1992 - Externalism and Inference]]; [[Boghossian 1994 - The Transparency of Mental Content]]). Under `D-09`, slow-switching equivocation directly conflicts with `APLA` ([[Slow-Switch Equivocation vs. Token-Wide APLA]]). Later externalists resist this scope assumption. [[Manuel Pérez Otero]] distinguishes token-level `APLA` (`D-09`) from form-level `APLA` (`D-10`), arguing that thinkers retain a priori knowledge of abstract validity schemas even when empirical facts determine whether two thought tokens instantiate the same term—and noting that Peter's predicament arises across internalist and externalist semantic theories alike ([[Otero 2012 - Boghossian's Inference Argument against Content Externalism Reversed]]).

In his reply, Boghossian concedes that abstract form-level logic (`D-10`) is unthreatened and that Peter's puzzle generalizes beyond proper names to natural-kind terms like `water`, yet contends that a narrow syntactic or psychological-mode level (`D-11`) is still needed to account for Peter's rational preparedness when reasoning across switched tokens ([[Boghossian 2014 - Reply to Otero's Boghossian's Inference Argument against Content Externalism Reversed]]). Related discussions by [[Paulo Faria]] and [[John M. Collins]] reinforce the split between `D-09` and `D-10`, stressing that while elementary patterns like *modus ponens* are known a priori, a particular token inference's validity depends on empirical co-reference conditions that cannot be discharged a priori ([[Faria 2009 - Unsafe reasoning A survey]]; [[Collins 2008 - Content Externalism and Brute Logical Error]]).
