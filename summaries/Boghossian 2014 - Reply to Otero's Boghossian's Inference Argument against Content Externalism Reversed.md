# Boghossian 2014 - Reply to Otero's Boghossian's Inference Argument against Content Externalism Reversed

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Boghossian-2014-Reply to Otero's Boghossian's Inference Argument against Content Externalism Reversed-Philosophy and Phenomenological Research.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Boghossian-2014-Reply-to-Otero|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

Boghossian replies to Manuel Perez Otero's claim that failures of transparency challenge any theory of content and may favor externalism over internalism. He agrees that the general challenge is not exclusive to externalism, but rejects Otero's emphasis on singular terms and argues that general terms prevent externalists from answering the problem merely by excluding the relevant thoughts from assessments of rationality. He also contends that a narrow, syntactic individuation of Peter's thoughts supplies internalists with a plausible explanation of Peter's rationality, so Otero has not shown externalist solutions to be superior.
Source: PDF pp. 1-3.

## Main Summary

Boghossian recalls his earlier argument that epistemic transparency about whether thoughts exercise the same or different concepts is central to rational agency and rational explanation. Externalist theories permit failures of this transparency and thereby risk misdescribing when an agent mishandles available information rather than merely lacks information. Otero replies that the difficulty confronts every theory of content, that externalism may handle it better than internalism, and that the use of a proper name in Switched Peter strengthens externalist intuitions. Source: PDF pp. 1-2.

Boghossian answers that his earlier discussion deliberately covered general terms such as `water`, not only names such as `Pavarotti`. Beliefs involving names might be excluded from rationality assessments in the way de re beliefs already are, but extending that maneuver to general terms would leave too little for rationality assessments to concern. General-term transparency failures therefore block this selective-exclusion response. Source: PDF p. 2.

Finally, although externalistically individuated thoughts make Peter's inference equivocate between `water` and `twater`, a narrow syntactic individuation renders it valid and can explain Peter's rationality. Boghossian concedes that this approach has problems, but argues that Otero has not shown it worse than externalist anaphoric or logical-luck accounts. He accepts the theory-neutral challenge while rejecting Otero's stronger comparative claims. Source: PDF p. 3.

## Arguments

- [[Otero Comparative Reversal vs. Boghossian Narrow-Syntax Reply]] (`ARG-07`) — rebuts Otero's reversal via `P-61` (pp. 1–3)

### Selective Exclusion Fails for General Terms *(p. 2)*

1. If externalist individuation applies to general terms, then transparency failures arise for thoughts involving general terms.
2. Externalist individuation applies to general terms.
3. Transparency failures arise for thoughts involving general terms.
4. If transparency failures arise for thoughts involving general terms, then every selective-exclusion response to externalism's transparency failures excludes thoughts involving general terms from assessments of rationality.
5. Every selective-exclusion response to externalism's transparency failures excludes thoughts involving general terms from assessments of rationality.
6. No response that excludes thoughts involving general terms from assessments of rationality adequately solves externalism's transparency failures.
7. Therefore, no selective-exclusion response to externalism's transparency failures adequately solves externalism's transparency failures.

### Narrow Syntactic Validity Explains Peter's Rational Preparedness *(p. 3)*

1. Under narrow syntactic individuation of mental representations, Peter's slow-switched inference is syntactically valid.
2. If under narrow syntactic individuation of mental representations Peter's slow-switched inference is syntactically valid, then a priori sensitivity to narrow syntactic validity independently explains slow-switched thinkers' rational preparedness.
3. Therefore, a priori sensitivity to narrow syntactic validity independently explains slow-switched thinkers' rational preparedness.
