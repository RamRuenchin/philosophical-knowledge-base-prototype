# Morvarid 2021 - A new argument for the incompatibility of content externalism with justification internalism

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Morvarid-2021-A New Argument for the Incompatibility of Content Externalism with Justification Internalism-Synthese.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Morvarid-2021-A New Argument for the Incompatibility of Content Externalism with Justification Internalism-Synthese|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

Several lines of reasoning have been proposed to show the incompatibility of content externalism with justification internalism. In this paper I examine two such lines of reasoning, which both rely on the general idea that since content externalism is incompatible with certain aspects of the alleged privileged character of self-knowledge, it would tend to undermine justification internalism as well. I shall argue that both lines of reasoning, as they stand, lack plausibility, though the core idea of the second line can be reconstructed into a new argument which shows considerable promise. In particular, relying upon the reliability constraint on knowledge, I shall argue that the so-called ‘two-concept’ version of content externalism is incompatible with ‘the transparency of sameness of content’, and thereby would also undermine justification internalism.
Source: p. 2333.

## Main Summary

Morvarid distinguishes two debates commonly described as internalism versus externalism. Content externalism says that mental content is partly individuated by a subject’s physical or social environment; justification internalism says that justificatory status depends on factors to which the subject has special access. The paper asks whether content externalism threatens accessibilist versions of justification internalism because it undermines aspects of privileged self-knowledge. Morvarid evaluates two proposed routes. The first moves from the alleged failure of privileged access to thought content; the second moves from failures of transparency concerning sameness of content. He argues that neither existing route succeeds as stated, but that the second contains the basis of a stronger conditional incompatibility argument. Source: pp. 2333–2334.

### 1 Preliminaries

Content externalism is introduced through Twin Earth cases in which intrinsic duplicates possess different contents because their environments differ. Morvarid then distinguishes three accessibilist formulations of justification internalism. Supervenience on the accessible (SA) says that justification supervenes on facts knowable by reflection alone. J-factor accessibility (JFA), the paper’s principal target, says that a subject is justified only if she can know by reflection alone that at least one sufficient justification-conferring factor for her belief obtains. Access internalism (AI) says that a subject can always acquire reflective knowledge of her justificatory status. Because a belief may have independently sufficient grounds, JFA requires reflective access to at least one J-factor, not all of them. Source: pp. 2334–2336.

Morvarid also separates privileged access (PA)—reflective knowledge of the content of one’s current thought—from transparency of sameness of content (TSC)—reflective knowledge that two current thoughts or constituents have the same content. This distinction structures the paper. The first argument claims that content externalism threatens justification internalism by undermining PA. The second appeals instead to content externalism’s conflict with TSC. Morvarid grants the first argument’s controversial premise for dialectical purposes, while proposing that the second route can be reconstructed more successfully. Source: pp. 2336–2337.

### 2 The privileged access argument explained

The privileged access argument assumes that content externalism undermines PA, as slow-switching or McKinsey-style reasoning is meant to show. In a slow-switching case, Oscar is now on Earth, perceptually forms the justified belief that there is water in the glass (*p*), and competently infers the justified disjunction *p* or *q*. The proposed reductio says that content externalism prevents Oscar from reflectively knowing that he believes *p*. JFA then appears to deny him justification for *p* or *q*, because its apparent J-factor consists of his justified belief that *p* together with the logical entailment from *p* to *p* or *q*. This contradicts the intuitive premise that his disjunctive belief is justified. A parallel argument uses Oscar’s perceptual appearance as the inaccessible wide-content J-factor for his perceptual belief. Source: pp. 2337–2340.

The argument generalizes beyond inference: whenever a belief’s justification depends on a belief, experience, memory, or intuition with externally individuated content, the conjunction of content externalism and JFA appears to make that justification inaccessible. On a slow-switching rationale, the result would concern switching subjects’ first-order wide-content beliefs; on a McKinsey rationale, it would threaten justification for wide-content beliefs much more generally. Morvarid nevertheless treats this as only the initial case, not a successful incompatibility proof. Source: p. 2340.

### 3 The privileged access argument criticized

The crucial defect is that the argument assumes the content-explicit condition is Oscar’s only J-factor. Morvarid proposes a second J-factor, H, stated in terms of demonstratively identified belief tokens: token *b*1 is a belief in Oscar’s mind, *b*1 is justified, token *b*2 is a belief in his mind, and *b*1’s content logically entails *b*2’s content. Reflective access to H could justify *b*2 even if Oscar cannot reflectively identify either belief’s specific wide content. Oscar can introspectively demonstrate each conscious token and judge that it is a belief without attributing a particular content to it. Standard McKinsey reasoning does not threaten such content-neutral second-order judgments, because they do not generate substantive a priori environmental knowledge. Source: pp. 2341–2343.

Slow-switching reasoning threatens those judgments only with further controversial assumptions. If the discrimination constraint on knowledge (DCK) and content essentialism are both true, Oscar’s actual token *b*1 cannot exist in the relevant Twin-Earth alternative with a different content; his inability to discriminate that alternative would then defeat knowledge that *b*1 is a belief in his mind. If content essentialism is rejected, however, the token may persist across the alternative and the second-order judgment remains true there. Morvarid therefore concludes that content externalism’s bearing on introspective access to the token depends on disputed theses rather than following from externalism alone. Source: pp. 2343–2344.

The remaining parts of H do not rescue the original PA-based route. Whether Oscar can know that *b*1 is justified raises the general hyper-intellectualization problem for internalism, not a distinctive problem created by content externalism. More importantly, knowing that *b*1’s content entails *b*2’s content requires knowing that the relevant content constituents are the same, even if Oscar need not know what those contents are. Any externalist obstacle here therefore arises from a failure of TSC, not a failure of PA. Consequently, the privileged access argument works only if it adds both DCK and content essentialism, or if it collapses into the separate transparency argument. Source: pp. 2344–2346.

### 4 The transparency argument

Morvarid first rejects Brown’s non-Fregean transparency argument. Brown’s coriander/cilantro case assumes that justification for a belief with content *p* automatically reduces justification for another belief with content not-*p*, even when the subject is inculpably ignorant that the contents conflict. Non-Fregean externalists must deny this assumption: Lois Lane may rationally and simultaneously believe that Superman is courageous and that Clark Kent is not courageous while not knowing that the names co-refer. Likewise, Rudolph’s justified cilantro beliefs need not diminish his coriander belief when he rationally does not know that the concepts are the same. Brown therefore has not shown an inaccessible change in justificatory status. Source: pp. 2346–2348.

The stronger argument begins with the two-concept construal of slow switching. A switched subject preserves a pre-switch concept while acquiring a post-switch concept, so Oscar may possess both water and twater concepts without reflectively knowing their difference. This defeats transparency of difference of content (TDC). Morvarid argues that it also defeats TSC. Because Oscar’s switching environment makes indistinguishable same-content and different-content judgments, he repeatedly judges falsely that distinct constituents have the same content. Even when he later makes a true same-content judgment, the introspective process is unreliable in relevant nearby cases, much as Henry’s barn judgment fails to be knowledge in fake-barn country. The reliability constraint on knowledge thus prevents Oscar’s true judgment from counting as knowledge, so two-concept externalism undermines TSC whether externalism is Fregean or non-Fregean. Source: pp. 2348–2349.

Returning to Oscar’s justified beliefs *b*1 (*p*) and *b*2 (*p* or *q*), Morvarid observes that both available J-factors require reflective access to the sameness of the water-content tokenings. J requires access to the proposition that the justified belief *p* entails *p* or *q*; H requires access to the proposition that *b*1’s content entails *b*2’s content. If two-concept externalism undermines TSC, Oscar cannot reflectively know either entailment and hence cannot reflectively access either J-factor. JFA therefore classifies the intuitively justified *b*2 as unjustified, yielding the desired incompatibility. Unlike the PA argument, this transparency argument does not assume that externalism defeats knowledge of one’s thought contents; unlike Brown’s argument, it does not assume unnoticed logical conflict automatically diminishes justification. Its conclusion is conditional on the independently disputed two-concept interpretation of switching. Source: pp. 2349–2350.

### 5 Conclusion

The PA-based argument is unsuccessful unless it either adopts controversial epistemological and metaphysical assumptions or becomes a TSC-based argument. Earlier implementations of the TSC route also fail, but the two-concept view plus a reliability constraint supports a more promising result: two-concept content externalism undermines TSC, making Oscar’s J-factors reflectively inaccessible and thereby conflicting with JFA. Morvarid presents this as a substantial conditional threat to justification internalism, not as an unconditional refutation of every combination of content externalism and internalism. Source: pp. 2350–2351.
