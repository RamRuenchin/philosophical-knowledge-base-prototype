# Otero 2012 - Boghossian's Inference Argument against Content Externalism Reversed

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Otero-2012-Boghossian's Inference Argument against Content Externalism Reversed-Philosophy and Phenomenological Research.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Otero-2012-Boghossian's Inference Argument against Content Externalism Reversed-Philosophy and Phenomenological Research|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

I deal here with one of Boghossian’s arguments against content externalism, related to our inferential rationality (to use his term). According to his reasoning, the apriority of our logical abilities is inconsistent with certain externalist assumptions. Nevertheless, the problem constitutes an important challenge for any theory of content, not just for externalism. Furthermore, when we examine what internalists may propose to solve the problem, we see that externalists have at their disposal a more promising repertoire of possible replies than internalists. In that sense, insofar as Boghossian’s scenario is relevant to the debate externalism ⁄ internalism, it can be seen (against Boghossian’s original intention) as providing additional evidence for content externalism.

Source: p. 159.

## Main Summary

### Introduction

Otero situates Boghossian’s inference argument among incompatibilist challenges alleging that content externalism conflicts with privileged self-knowledge. He accepts both linguistic and intentional externalism while retaining a substantive, perhaps modest, form of privileged access to thought. Boghossian’s particular challenge concerns the alleged a priority of logical abilities: the claim that thinkers know a priori whether their own inferences are logically valid. Otero’s primary thesis is that the problematic case challenges every theory of content, not only externalism. His stronger thesis is comparative: once the available internalist and externalist responses are examined, externalism has the more promising repertoire. He tentatively explains this advantage by noting that the case concerns a singular term, for which externalist intuitions may be especially strong. Source: pp. 160–161.

### 1. Peter’s Inference and Boghossian’s Challenge

Boghossian asks us to assume a language of thought and constructs a slow-switch case. Peter first encounters Pavarotti on Earth, is unknowingly transported to Twin Earth, and eventually uses “Pavarotti” in ways normally connected to twin Pavarotti. Yet memory still enables thoughts about the original Pavarotti. Peter then infers, from “Pavarotti once swam in Lake Taupo” and “The singer I heard yesterday is Pavarotti,” that the singer heard yesterday once swam in Lake Taupo. On the externalist description, the first occurrence of “Pavarotti” concerns the Earth singer while the second concerns his twin, so true premises yield a false conclusion through unnoticed equivocation. Source: pp. 161–162.

Boghossian claims that this conflicts with APLA, the principle that a subject can know a priori whether a particular inference is logically valid. APLA is stronger than a priori knowledge that an abstract argument form is valid: it requires the thinker to know whether particular premise and conclusion tokens instantiate such a form. Peter’s reasoning appears transparently valid to him, although external individuation allegedly makes it invalid in a way he cannot detect. Otero argues that any account of content must explain this case; the appearance that the difficulty derives specifically from externalism becomes doubtful once internalist explanations are compared. Source: pp. 162–163.

### 2. Some Externalist Solutions

The anaphorically based externalist solution, associated with Schiffer and Burge, holds that Peter’s inference is valid. The intention to preserve reference across the reasoning constrains the second occurrence of “Pavarotti” to refer back to the Earth singer, much as an anaphor inherits its antecedent’s referent. Premise P2 and the conclusion are therefore false, but the inference remains valid and APLA survives. Brown objects that this makes thought content depend counterintuitively on premise order; Boghossian objects that Peter could bring independently grounded memory beliefs together. Otero replies that cognitive “putting together” may itself be governed by the same anaphoric restrictions and notes that other reasoning contexts already show order-sensitive interpretation. Source: pp. 163–165.

The logical-luck solution, defended by Sorensen and Faria, instead accepts that the inference is invalid and abandons APLA. Rationality and success in following a valid pattern can depend on factors outside the thinker’s access and control, just as moral luck makes moral assessment depend partly on luck. This view entails a radical inferential externalism and conflicts with the intuition that rationality is internally accessible, but Otero emphasizes that APLA is unusually demanding; its rejection need not undermine the weaker ability to distinguish valid from invalid forms. Brown’s weaker-rationality account and Garmendia’s proposal to supply an implicit identity premise are further options, but Otero regards the anaphoric and logical-luck strategies as the least problematic externalist responses. Source: pp. 165–167.

### 3. Two Internalist Accounts, Implying Identity in Content

Otero next examines internalist attempts to preserve a common meaning for the two occurrences of “Pavarotti.” On the Same-Meaning/Same-Unified-Reference view, “Pavarotti” has one concept and one unified referent encompassing Earth and Twin-Earth uses, making both premises and the conclusion true and preserving APLA. The price is an extravagant semantics or ontology on which the two singers count as the same singer. The view also sits uneasily with Boghossian’s own assertion that Peter’s inference is invalid, though that assertion might be read as part of the externalist description rather than Boghossian’s settled view. Source: pp. 168–170.

The Same-Meaning/Same-Disjunctive-Reference view treats “Pavarotti” as an amalgam concept covering two distinct singers, analogously to “jade” covering jadeite and nephrite. If P2 is interpreted as an identity claim, P2 and the conclusion are false but the inference may remain valid; if “is” expresses inclusion, P2 is true while the conclusion is false and APLA fails. Either way, it is highly counterintuitive to treat a paradigmatic singular term as disjunctively referring to two people. Thus these identity-in-content strategies secure some desired epistemic result only by sacrificing strong semantic or ontological intuitions. Source: pp. 170–172.

### 4. Indexical Internalist Content

Three further internalist proposals model “Pavarotti” as indexical. Same-Indexical-Meaning/Different-Reference assigns it a stable rule-like meaning but lets context determine different referents on Earth and Twin Earth. That makes Peter’s premises true and conclusion false, so it violates APLA, and it implausibly treats a proper name as functioning like a role expression. Same-Context-Imprinting-Indexical-Meaning/Same-Reference fixes the referent through the first context of use, rendering P2 false and preserving validity and APLA, but depends on a category of context-imprinting indexicals whose application to ordinary proper names conflicts with pre-theoretical judgment. Source: pp. 172–174.

Same-Indexical-Meaning/Same-Anaphorically-Based-Reference combines indexical meaning with the anaphoric constraint: within Peter’s inference, the second token inherits the first token’s reference, so the inference is valid and APLA is retained. Otero regards this as the strongest of the five same-content internalist options, but it inherits both major objections to the externalist anaphoric solution—order sensitivity and Boghossian’s “putting together” objection—while adding the implausibility of classifying proper names as indexicals. Its comparative disadvantage supports Otero’s stronger thesis that externalist replies are better positioned. Source: pp. 174–175.

### 5. Different Content, Internally Determined

The final internalist proposal accepts different contents for the two occurrences of “Pavarotti” but says that the difference is fixed by internally accessible factors. Peter’s inference is then invalid. An internalist might deny that a rational Peter would actually make it, but Otero finds this empirically implausible because Peter’s observable conduct would strongly suggest that he does. A more cautious version allows the mistake while maintaining that sufficient reflection would reveal the content difference and enable Peter to avoid it, thereby preserving APLA as an accessible capacity rather than a guarantee of actual performance. Yet the proposal faces two serious problems: it is unclear what internally accessible factors could differentiate the contents, and the externalist intuitions behind Twin-Earth cases are especially compelling for singular terms. Source: pp. 176–177.

### 6. Another Externalist Solution

If the internalist can identify such internal differentiating factors, a non-Millian externalist can use them too while also allowing external reference to help determine meaning. Otero calls this Different-Internally-and-Externally-Determined-Meaning. Standard externalism does not say that external factors exhaust meaning; only radical Millian externalism identifies a proper name’s meaning solely with its referent. Thus any workable internally differentiated account is also available to the non-Millian externalist, which can match its advantages without surrendering external determination. If no suitable internal factors exist, the corresponding internalist proposal fails as well. Millian externalists, meanwhile, can still appeal to the anaphoric or logical-luck solutions. Source: pp. 177–179.

### Conclusions

Otero concludes that Boghossian’s case does not expose a difficulty generated specifically by externalism. Every internalist strategy either gives up APLA, revises ordinary semantics or ontology in counterintuitive ways, inherits the externalist strategy’s problems while adding new ones, or depends on internal differentiators that a non-Millian externalist can equally invoke. The minimal conclusion is therefore that Peter’s inference challenges any theory of content. The stronger conclusion is that externalism is comparatively better placed: its best replies are at least as good as their internalist rivals, and the ordinary intuitions strained by the internalist accounts are themselves externalist intuitions. Because the example uses a singular term, Boghossian’s attempted argument against externalism may inadvertently supply especially vivid evidence against content internalism. Source: pp. 179–180.
