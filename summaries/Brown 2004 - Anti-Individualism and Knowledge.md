# Brown 2004 - Anti-Individualism and Knowledge

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Brown-2004-Anti Individualism and Knowledge-MIT Press.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Brown-2004-Anti Individualism and Knowledge-MIT Press/index|Index]] / [[converted_markdown/Brown-2004-Anti Individualism and Knowledge-MIT Press/complete|Complete]]

## Abstract

Jessica Brown examines the epistemological consequences of anti-individualism (externalism about mental content)—the view that a subject's thought contents are partly individuated by relations to her physical and social environment. Addressing the three major challenges posed to anti-individualism in contemporary philosophy of mind, Brown argues that anti-individualism lacks the radical epistemological consequences commonly attributed to it:
1. *The Achievement Problem (Knowledge of Mind)*: Anti-individualism does not undermine privileged access (a priori self-knowledge of one's own thoughts). The discrimination argument fails because knowing what one thinks does not require distinguishing actual thoughts from counterfactual switch scenarios that are not relevant alternatives. For views admitting illusions of thought (no-reference cases), global and local reliability are defended via the relevant alternatives framework.
2. *Anti-Individualism and Reasoning*: Anti-individualism is incompatible with the transparency of content (the claim that sameness and difference of content are graspable a priori). However, this does not undermine rationality once we reject the idealized conception of a rational agent as an a priori logician. Crucially, this failure of transparency creates a profound tension between anti-individualism and Fregean senses (even modern object-dependent senses), showing that anti-individualism cannot readily co-opt Fregean explanations of cognitive significance.
3. *The Consequence Problem (Knowledge of the World)*: Anti-individualism combined with privileged access does not generate an absurd a priori route to knowledge of the external world (the McKinsey reductio). Rejecting Davies's and Wright's limitation principles on warrant transmission, Brown shows instead that anti-individualism yields no a priori knowable entailments from thought contents to specific environmental facts that a subject can know a priori.
Source: pp. ix-xiv, 1-32, 309-312 (PDF: pp. 11-16, 19-50, 343-345).

### Preface


### 1 Anti-Individualism

#### Section Abstract

Jessica Brown introduces the core distinctions and taxonomy of anti-individualism (externalism about mental content) that structure the monograph. Contrasting anti-individualism with individualism via supervenience on intrinsic physical states, she delineates three varieties of anti-individualism: natural kind, singular, and social. To defend these against individualist resistance, she distinguishes descriptive from nondescriptive reference, showing that individualists cannot assimilate ordinary thought to descriptive thought. She introduces two crucial cross-cutting axes: the divide between descriptive and illusion versions of anti-individualism in no-reference cases, and the divide between Fregean (object-dependent sense) and non-Fregean anti-individualism. Finally, she clarifies the moderate epistemic conception of "a priori" knowledge used throughout the work (knowledge without justificatory reliance on perceptual experience of the external world) and maps the book's overarching dialectic.
Source: pp. 1-32 (PDF: pp. 19-50).

#### Main Summary

##### 1 Content and the Environment

Propositional attitudes—such as belief, desire, hope, and fear—comprise an attitude directed toward a propositional content, specifying how a subject takes or desires the world to be. Such contents are essential for predicting and explaining rational action. While individualists and anti-individualists agree that mental states are causally linked to the environment via perception and action, they diverge over the metaphysical individuation of content. Individualism holds that a subject's thought contents are wholly individuated by her intrinsic, nonrelational properties (such as neurophysiological brain states); thought content strictly supervenes on intrinsic physical states, so that duplicate intrinsic states necessitate duplicate contents. Anti-individualism denies this supervenience thesis, holding that thought contents are partly individuated by relations to the subject's physical and social environment.
Source: pp. 1-5 (PDF: pp. 19-23).

To evaluate anti-individualist arguments, Brown distinguishes two models of singular and kind reference: the descriptive model and the nondescriptive model. On the descriptive model, a subject thinks of an object or kind via a general condition or description (e.g., "the person responsible for the High Street burglaries"). The content of a descriptive thought is independent of which particular item (if any) fits the description; across counterfactual changes in the referent, content remains invariant. On the nondescriptive model, thought connects directly to objects or kinds in virtue of real epistemic and perceptual relations (e.g., seeing an apple or possessing a recognitional capacity for an acquaintance). Attempts to reduce perceptual demonstratives or recognition-based thoughts to descriptions fail because uniquely identifying descriptions either surreptitiously embed demonstratives or impose cognitively implausible conceptual sophistication.
Source: pp. 5-9 (PDF: pp. 23-27).

##### 2 Natural Kind Anti-Individualism

Natural kind anti-individualism maintains that thought contents are partly individuated by the physical and biological natural kinds in the thinker's environment. Natural kinds are individuated by fundamental microstructural properties discovered by science rather than superficial sensory appearance. In Putnam's classic Twin Earth thought experiment, Earth Sally (prior to the discovery of chemistry) regularly interacts with water (H2O), intending her term "water" to express a concept applying to whatever shares that fundamental nature. On Twin Earth, duplicate Sally possesses an identical history of intrinsic physical and phenomenal states, but her environment contains XYZ ("twater"). Natural kind anti-individualists hold that Earth Sally expresses the concept *water* (applying to H2O), whereas Twin Sally expresses the distinct concept *twater* (applying to XYZ). Because intrinsic duplicates possess distinct contents solely in virtue of environmental differences, content does not supervene on intrinsic states.
Source: pp. 9-11 (PDF: pp. 27-29).

Individualists attempt to block this conclusion by proposing that kind terms express descriptions. Obvious descriptions based on superficial properties fail because speakers intend natural kind terms to track underlying essences rather than mere appearance. Theoretical descriptions (e.g., "the chemical substance H2O") fail because speakers are ignorant of chemistry. A two-dimensional description embedding "actually" (e.g., "the liquid that actually flows in rivers around here") yields extension shifts across worlds, but is psychologically implausible as an account of ordinary speakers' concepts, since communities successfully refer to natural kinds through recognitional abilities even while holding radically false descriptive beliefs.
Source: pp. 11-13 (PDF: pp. 29-31).

##### 3 Singular Anti-Individualism

Singular anti-individualism holds that thought contents are partly individuated by the particular objects in the subject's environment. When Sally perceives apple *a* and thinks "that apple is red," the singular component of her thought refers directly to *a*, and its truth-value depends specifically on *a*. In a counterfactual scenario where Sally possesses identical intrinsic brain states and retinal stimulations but looks at indistinguishable apple *b*, her thought refers to *b*. Because the thought connects nondescriptively to its referent, the content of the singular thought varies between the two scenarios despite complete intrinsic identity. Thus, singular thoughts are externally individuated by the particular objects perceived.
Source: pp. 13-15 (PDF: pp. 31-33).

##### 4 Illusions of Thought

Beyond twin cases where a different object or kind is present, anti-individualists must address no-reference cases where no suitable object or kind exists at all (e.g., hallucinations, or Boghossian's "Dry Earth" where watery liquid is an illusion, or "Motley Earth" where apparent water is an unstable mixture of multiple distinct kinds). Two divergent anti-individualist responses exist:
1. *Descriptive Version*: In no-reference situations, the subject entertains a fallback descriptive thought (e.g., "the apple I seem to see is red" or "the clear liquid called 'water' is wet"). Content remains available even when reference fails.
2. *Illusion Version*: When the requisite environmental relation fails, the subject fails to think any determinate thought of the intended type. Although it seems subjectively to the thinker that she is having a thought, she in fact suffers an "illusion of thought" (Evans, McDowell, Boghossian).
Brown leaves both options open as viable forms of anti-individualism whose distinct epistemological profiles must be analyzed.
Source: pp. 15-17 (PDF: pp. 33-35).

##### 5 Social Anti-Individualism

Social anti-individualism holds that a subject's thought contents are partly individuated by the linguistic practices and conventions of her social community. Burge's arthritis thought experiment supports this thesis: Sally has numerous true beliefs about arthritis in her ankles and wrists, but mistakenly believes that her arthritis has spread to her thigh. Despite her incomplete understanding (since arthritis is medically defined as an ailment strictly of the joints), ordinary practice and Sally's own linguistic deference attribute to her the public concept *arthritis*. In a counterfactual community where "arthritis" is standardly defined to encompass rheumatoid ailments of both joints and thighs ("tharthritis"), intrinsic duplicate Sally would express the concept *tharthritis*. Because the sole difference is social-linguistic usage, mental content depends on social environment.
Source: pp. 17-20 (PDF: pp. 35-38).

Burge defends this attribution against the individualist reinterpretation (which claims Sally has an idiosyncratic concept like *tharthritis* in both cases) by emphasizing linguistic deference. If Sally's doctor informs her that arthritis cannot occur in thighs, Sally experiences relief, abandons her fear, and acknowledges her error; she does not insist that she had an idiosyncratic concept under which her belief was true.
Source: pp. 19-20 (PDF: pp. 37-38).

##### 6 Fregean and Non-Fregean Anti-Individualism

A major theoretical divide separates non-Fregean anti-individualism (which rejects senses and treats singular/kind contents as directly referential) from Fregean anti-individualism (which retains Fregean modes of presentation to explain informative identity statements, cognitive significance, and rational action). While traditional descriptive conceptions of sense conflict with anti-individualism, modern Fregean anti-individualists (Evans, McDowell, Peacocke) introduce *object-dependent senses*—modes of presentation that cannot be entertained in the absence of the object. Brown identifies this synthesis as an attractive position that promises to combine externalist individuation with Fregean cognitive explanations, setting up the critical evaluation of its coherence in Chapter 6.
Source: pp. 20-22 (PDF: pp. 38-40).

##### 7 A Priori and Empirical

Brown clarifies the epistemic terminology governing the book's inquiry. A proposition is known a priori if it is known without justificatory reliance on perceptual experience of the external world, even if empirical experience was temporally necessary to acquire the constituent concepts (e.g., knowing that red is a color). While narrow views restrict the a priori to logic and mathematics, Brown adopts the widely accepted moderate conception in the self-knowledge literature (McKinsey, Boghossian, Warfield, McLaughlin, Burge), according to which a subject can have a priori (or non-empirical) knowledge of her own conscious thoughts without investigating the external environment.
Source: pp. 22-25 (PDF: pp. 40-43).

##### 8 Outline of the Book

Brown outlines the three overarching epistemological problems addressed in the monograph:
1. *The Compatibility of Anti-Individualism and Privileged Access (Chapters 2-4)*: Addressing the discrimination argument (the challenge that subjects cannot discriminate actual thoughts from slow-switch twin thoughts) and the illusion argument (the challenge from empty/hallucinatory cases). Brown argues against standard reliabilist solutions and against Evans's discriminative-ability account of thought, developing instead a relevant-alternatives solution.
2. *Anti-Individualism and Rationality (Chapters 5-6)*: Investigating whether the loss of transparency of sameness and difference of content undermines rationality, and demonstrating that the resulting conception of rationality generates a fatal tension with Fregean sense.
3. *Anti-Individualism and A Priori Knowledge of the World (Chapters 7-8)*: Evaluating the McKinsey reductio. Brown refutes Wright's and Davies's transmission-of-warrant restrictions and demonstrates that anti-individualism provides no a priori knowable entailments to external environmental facts.
Source: pp. 25-32 (PDF: pp. 43-50).

#### Important Concepts

- **Anti-individualism (content externalism).**
  Definition: The thesis that the contents of a subject's propositional attitudes are partly individuated by relations to her physical, social, or historical environment, such that thought content does not supervene on the subject's intrinsic or microstructural properties.
  Source: pp. 1-2, 21-22 (PDF: pp. 19-20, 39-40).
  Other usage: Contrasted with individualism (internalism), which holds that thought contents are wholly determined by intrinsic physical states.

- **Descriptive model vs. nondescriptive model of thought.**
  Definition: The descriptive model holds that singular or kind reference is mediated by general descriptions or satisfaction conditions (`the F`), leaving content invariant across referential shifts; the nondescriptive model holds that reference is established directly via real perceptual, causal, or recognitional relations that partly individuate content.
  Source: pp. 5-9 (PDF: pp. 23-27).

- **Descriptive version vs. illusion version of anti-individualism.**
  Definition: In no-reference situations (where an intended object or kind is absent or an illusion), the descriptive version maintains that the subject entertains an alternative descriptive thought, whereas the illusion version holds that the subject suffers an illusion of thought and entertains no determinate thought content.
  Source: pp. 15-17 (PDF: pp. 33-35).

- **Fregean anti-individualism (object-dependent sense).**
  Definition: An anti-individualist framework holding that thoughts involve Fregean modes of presentation, where those senses are object-dependent: a way of thinking about an object that is unavailable to be entertained in the absence of the object.
  Source: pp. 20-22 (PDF: pp. 38-40).

#### Formal-Logical Argument Reconstruction: Twin Earth Argument for Natural Kind Anti-Individualism
1. In the actual environment (Earth), Sally has an intrinsic physical state $S$ and interacts with liquid H2O, thereby expressing the concept *water* which applies exclusively to H2O. Source: pp. 9-11 (PDF: pp. 27-29).
2. In a counterfactual environment (Twin Earth), Sally possesses the identical intrinsic physical state $S$ but interacts with liquid XYZ ("twater"), thereby expressing the concept *twater* which applies exclusively to XYZ. Source: pp. 10-11 (PDF: pp. 28-29).
3. If two subjects in identical intrinsic physical states possess different thought contents in virtue of differing environmental relations, then thought contents do not supervene wholly on intrinsic physical states and are partly individuated by the environment. (implicit)
4. Therefore, thought contents are partly individuated by the natural kinds in the subject's environment (natural kind anti-individualism). Source: pp. 10-11 (PDF: pp. 28-29) (from 1-3).

### 2 Knowledge and Discrimination

#### Section Abstract

Jessica Brown examines the discrimination argument—the primary incompatibilist challenge alleging that anti-individualism undermines privileged access (a priori knowledge of one's own thought contents). The argument holds that knowledge requires discriminative abilities, yet a subject cannot distinguish a priori between her actual thought and relevant alternative thoughts she would have in slow-switch twin environments. Brown critiques the standard compatibilist defense (Burge, Heil, Falvey and Owens), which appeals to the reliability of self-verifying "cogito" thoughts. She demonstrates that reliable belief-formation does not entail discriminative ability: a slow-switched subject possesses reliable second-order beliefs but cannot notice conceptual change, cannot make accurate comparative sameness/difference judgments, and cannot act differentially across general behavioral tasks. Furthermore, she shows that classic epistemological defenses of reliabilism (Goldman's fake barn case) conflate reliability with discriminative capacity. Finally, examining alternative compatibilist models (Falvey & Owens, McLaughlin & Tye), Brown demonstrates that neither shows that knowledge requires only reliability nor that anti-individualism preserves discriminative abilities, necessitating alternative strategies that finesse this epistemological impasse.
Source: pp. 33-73 (PDF: pp. 53-93).

#### Main Summary

##### 1 Privileged Access

A subject possesses direct, non-empirical epistemological access to her own mental states, in sharp contrast to third-person knowledge of others' minds. While knowing what another person thinks requires perceptual observation and inference from linguistic or bodily behavior, a subject knows her own mundane beliefs, desires, hopes, and intentions without observing her behavior and without consulting evidence about her past conduct. Although exceptions occur in self-deception and repressed unconscious attitudes, ordinary first-person self-knowledge does not depend on perceptual experience of the external world. Following standard usage in the self-knowledge literature, Brown characterizes this non-empirical knowledge as "a priori": knowledge that lacks justificatory reliance on perceptual experience of the external world, even if empirical experience was temporally necessary to acquire the concepts involved.
Source: pp. 33-37 (PDF: pp. 53-57).

Anti-individualism appears to threaten this privileged access. If a subject's thoughts are partly individuated by environmental factors about which she may be ignorant or mistaken (such as chemical compositions or communal linguistic practices), it seems she could know what she thinks only by investigating those external factors. For instance, knowing that she thinks *water is wet* would seem to require empirical chemical analysis of the local liquid. Because anti-individualists virtually all concede that making self-knowledge contingent on chemical or linguistic field investigation is an unacceptable consequence, the compatibility of anti-individualism and privileged access constitutes a paramount problem.
Source: pp. 36-37 (PDF: pp. 56-57).

##### 2 The Discrimination Argument

The discrimination argument articulates this threat by exploiting the intuitive epistemological connection between knowledge and discriminative abilities. In perceptual epistemology, a subject looking at a crow does not know it is a crow if she cannot visually discriminate crows from rooks that are abundant in the vicinity. Analogously, the incompatibilist argues that a subject cannot know a priori that she is thinking that *p* if there is a relevant alternative situation in which she lacks that thought, yet which she cannot distinguish a priori from her actual situation.
Source: pp. 37-43 (PDF: pp. 57-63).

To substantiate this argument without relying on far-fetched science-fiction scenarios, incompatibilists appeal to "slow switch" cases. Suppose Sally lives on Earth, where "water" refers to H2O. Unbeknownst to her, she is transported to Twin Earth, where the clear liquid is XYZ ("twater") and the term "water" expresses the concept *twater*. Over time, her environmental interactions shift the content of her thoughts: her post-switch utterances of "Water is wet" come to express thoughts about twater. Because the switch is gradual and seamless, Sally's subjective experience is identical; she notices no change in her thoughts. When asked whether her thoughts have changed, she would deny it. Incompatibilists conclude that Sally cannot distinguish a priori between the actual situation (thinking *water is wet*) and the counterfactual situation (thinking *twater is wet*). Because slow-switch possibilities are relevant for subjects who move between dialects or sub-communities where words differ in meaning (e.g., British vs. American English terms like "chicory", "chips", or "public school"), the alternative cannot be dismissed as an irrelevant skeptical fantasy.
Source: pp. 39-43 (PDF: pp. 59-63).

Compatibilists standardly respond by stressing the reliability of self-knowledge (Burge 1988; Heil 1988; Falvey & Owens 1994). They observe that the environment co-individuates both first-order thoughts and second-order thoughts about those thoughts. Specifically, Burge emphasizes self-verifying "cogito" thoughts: in forming the second-order belief "I believe that I think that water is wet," the subject inherently tokens the first-order content, guaranteeing truth. Even after switching, Sally cannot falsely believe she thinks *water is wet*; her second-order thoughts automatically track her new first-order thoughts (*twater is wet*). However, Brown points out a crucial gap: the discrimination argument concerns discriminative abilities, not mere reliable true belief. Stressing reliability does not answer the incompatibilist unless reliability either entails discriminative ability or suffices for knowledge without it.
Source: pp. 43-45 (PDF: pp. 63-65).

##### 3 Discrimination and Reliability

Investigating whether reliable belief-formation suffices for discriminative ability, Brown analyzes Goldman's classic epistemology. Goldman initially argued that knowledge that *p* requires the ability to distinguish the actual state of affairs from relevant alternative states in which *p* is false (Goldman 1976). However, in his later reliabilist formulations (Goldman 1986), he rephrased this requirement as a reliable belief-producing process. Brown demonstrates that this equation is mistaken: slow-switch cases prove that a reliable ability to form true beliefs is not sufficient for a discriminative ability.
Source: pp. 45-48 (PDF: pp. 65-68).

Discriminative abilities are systematically linked to three distinct cognitive capacities:
1. *Noticing change*: A subject who can discriminate between two types can detect when an object shifts from one type to another (e.g., detecting a color change from blue to yellow). Sally completely fails to notice her conceptual transition from water to twater thoughts.
2. *Reliable sameness/difference judgments*: A subject who discriminates two types can correctly judge whether two presented instances share the same type. Post-switch Sally falsely judges that her current thoughts have the same content as her pre-switch thoughts.
3. *Differential action across arbitrary repertoires*: A subject who discriminates two types can manifest this capacity across arbitrary behavioral tasks (e.g., press button A if blue, button B if yellow). If instructed to press a button if and only if she tokens a twater thought, Sally cannot succeed above chance without empirical information.
Source: pp. 48-51 (PDF: pp. 68-71).

Brown systematically refutes three compatibilist attempts to show that Sally retains discriminative abilities:
- *The utterance-content argument*: Arguing that Sally reports her thoughts using different concepts (uttering "water" to report water thoughts on Earth and twater thoughts on Twin Earth) fails because this vocal difference is externally imposed by the linguistic community without the subject noticing any difference.
- *The intentional-action argument*: Arguing that Sally acts differentially under intentional descriptions (reaching for water on Earth vs. reaching for twater on Twin Earth) fails because she cannot translate this difference into general behavioral tasks (such as pressing a button upon having a twater thought).
- *The memory-loss argument*: Arguing that Sally's failure to discriminate is merely a failure of memory (having forgotten her old water thoughts) fails because in ordinary cases of memory failure, discriminative capacity can be independently confirmed through accurate descriptions or simultaneous presentation, whereas Sally gives identical descriptions of both concepts and is blind to their chemical difference.
Source: pp. 51-59 (PDF: pp. 71-79).

##### 4 Knowledge and Reliability

Having shown that anti-individualism undermines discriminative abilities in slow-switch cases, Brown turns to the second compatibilist option: showing that knowledge requires only reliability and not discriminative capacity.
Source: pp. 59-60 (PDF: pp. 79-80).

She re-examines the foundational arguments for reliabilism, especially Goldman's fake barn case. In normal countryside, a driver who identifies a real barn has knowledge; in fake barn country, where indistinguishable paper-mache facsimiles abound, her true belief fails to constitute knowledge. Goldman uses this case to motivate reliabilism. However, Brown reveals a profound diagnostic ambiguity: in the fake barn case, possession of a reliable belief-forming process and possession of a discriminative capacity *go strictly hand in hand*. The driver in normal country is reliable *because* she can discriminate barns from cows and tractors; in fake barn country, she is unreliable *because* she cannot discriminate barns from fakes. The case therefore provides equal support for two rival epistemological analyses: one requiring reliable belief-formation, and one requiring discriminative capacity.
Source: pp. 60-64 (PDF: pp. 80-84).

The same hand-in-hand alignment appears in reliabilist responses to Cartesian skepticism (Brain-in-a-Vat scenarios). Because the standard epistemological literature has only examined cases where reliability and discriminative ability coincide, it has overlooked the distinction between them and cannot be cited as authoritative evidence that knowledge requires only reliability. The slow-switch scenario is unique precisely because reliable belief and discriminative capacity pull apart.
Source: pp. 62-64 (PDF: pp. 82-84).

##### 5 Other Compatibilist Responses—Falvey and Owens, and McLaughlin and Tye

Brown critiques two sophisticated contemporary compatibilist proposals designed to rescue privileged access under slow switching:
- *Falvey and Owens (1994)*: They distinguish two principles: (RA), which says an alternative undermines knowledge if it is compatible with the subject's evidence; and (RA'), which says an alternative undermines knowledge only if it would lead to a false belief. They defend (RA') by arguing that one who knows is simply reliable, so that relevant alternatives matter only when they induce false belief. Because Sally's cogito thoughts on Twin Earth are true (*I think that twater is wet*), (RA') permits knowledge. Brown refutes this defense on two grounds: first, Falvey and Owens provide no independent argument against the discriminative requirement (RA); second, McLaughlin and Tye's "sawdust" counterexample proves (RA') false. In the sawdust case, Oscar wonders whether a wizard replaced his brain with sawdust, consults chicken entrails, and forms the true belief that he has a brain. This belief is not knowledge, even though the sawdust alternative would not cause him to form a false belief (since a sawdust head lacks all beliefs). Thus, relevant alternatives can undermine knowledge without generating false beliefs.
Source: pp. 64-68 (PDF: pp. 84-88).
- *McLaughlin and Tye (1998)*: They defend compatibilism by accepting (RA) but arguing that the evidence for a second-order belief is the first-order thought itself, which reliably causes the second-order belief. Because thinking *water is wet* is logically incompatible with thinking a twater thought, the subject's evidence strictly rules out the twin alternative. Brown refutes this by showing that even if the causal ground of a belief is incompatible with an alternative, this does not confer discriminative ability. If a subject's true belief that a fruit is an apple is caused by the presence of an apple, the presence of the apple is incompatible with its being a nectarine; but if the subject's eyesight is too degraded to distinguish apples from nectarines, she still lacks visual discriminative capacity and lacks knowledge. Thus, McLaughlin and Tye fail to show that anti-individualism is compatible with the discriminative abilities demanded by incompatibilists.
Source: pp. 68-71 (PDF: pp. 88-91).

##### 6 Conclusion

Brown synthesizes the findings of Chapter 2. The discrimination argument cannot be dismissed merely by noting the reliability of cogito thoughts. Anti-individualism genuinely undermines a subject's ability to discriminate actual thoughts from relevant slow-switch twin thoughts. Furthermore, existing compatibilist arguments fail to establish that knowledge requires only reliability rather than discriminative capacity.
Source: pp. 71-73 (PDF: pp. 91-93).

Rather than remaining impassed in the debate over whether knowledge requires discriminative capacity, Brown proposes exploring strategies that *finesse* this epistemological dispute—strategies that defend compatibilism whether knowledge requires discriminative ability or only reliability. In Chapter 3, she evaluates the strategy of building discriminative requirements into the metaphysics of thought itself (Evans). In Chapter 4, she develops the strategy of showing that slow-switch twin situations are not relevant alternatives.
Source: pp. 72-73 (PDF: pp. 92-93).

#### Important Concepts

- **Privileged access.**
  Definition: The thesis that a subject can have direct a priori knowledge of her own propositional attitudes and thought contents without relying justificatorily on perceptual observation of her behavior or empirical investigation of her environment.
  Source: pp. 33-37 (PDF: pp. 53-57).

- **Discrimination argument.**
  Definition: An incompatibilist argument asserting that knowledge that *p* requires the ability to distinguish the actual situation from relevant alternative situations where *p* is false; since anti-individualism entails that subjects in slow-switch scenarios cannot distinguish actual from twin thoughts, privileged access is undermined.
  Source: pp. 37-45, 71-73 (PDF: pp. 57-65, 91-93).

- **Discriminative ability vs. reliable belief-formation.**
  Definition: A discriminative ability requires noticing changes between types, forming reliable comparative judgments of sameness and difference, and acting differentially across arbitrary behavioral repertoires; reliable belief-formation is the capacity of a belief-forming mechanism to yield true beliefs. In slow switching, the two capacities pull apart.
  Source: pp. 45-59, 59-64 (PDF: pp. 65-79, 79-84).

- **Self-verifying second-order thoughts (Burgean cogito thoughts).**
  Definition: Second-order thoughts of the form "I believe that I think that *p*," in which the act of forming the second-order thought tokens and ascribes the constituent first-order content, guaranteeing true belief even under slow-switch conditions.
  Source: pp. 43-44 (PDF: pp. 63-64).

#### Formal-Logical Argument Reconstruction: The Discrimination Argument against Compatibilism
1. A subject $S$ knows a priori that she thinks that $p$ only if $S$ can distinguish a priori the actual situation (in which she thinks that $p$) from relevant alternative situations in which she lacks that thought. Source: pp. 37-43, 45-47 (PDF: pp. 57-63, 65-67).
2. If anti-individualism is true, there are relevant alternative situations (such as slow-switch scenarios) in which $S$ lacks the thought that $p$ (entertaining instead a twin thought that $p_{twin}$) but which are subjectively indistinguishable from the actual situation. Source: pp. 37-41 (PDF: pp. 57-61).
3. If an alternative situation is subjectively indistinguishable from the actual situation, $S$ cannot distinguish between them a priori. (implicit)
4. Therefore, if anti-individualism is true, $S$ cannot know a priori that she thinks that $p$ (anti-individualism is incompatible with privileged access). Source: pp. 38, 40-41 (PDF: pp. 58, 60-61) (from 1-3).

### 3 Thought and Discriminative Abilities—Evans

#### Section Abstract

Jessica Brown examines a sophisticated compatibilist strategy that takes seriously the incompatibilist claim that knowledge requires discriminative abilities: building a discriminative requirement into the very metaphysical conditions for having a thought. Evaluating Gareth Evans's influential account of singular thought—according to which thinking about an object requires knowing which object it is by being able to distinguish it from all other objects—Brown assesses whether satisfying Evans's conditions enables a subject to distinguish her actual thought from relevant alternative thoughts. She demonstrates that Evans's account fails to solve the discrimination argument. First, it cannot rule out no-reference (illusion) alternatives. Second, in perceptual demonstratives, locating an object distinguishes it from other objects in the actual environment but fails to provide a priori discriminability from counterfactual twin scenarios where a duplicate occupies that same location. Third, for recognition-based thoughts, Evans's subjective "estimated area of search" yields counterintuitive epistemic results. Brown defends a modified account of recognitional capacities based on the *actual area of search*, which guarantees that no relevant duplicates exist within the search area, thereby providing a partial defense against twin alternatives that will be integrated into the relevant-alternatives framework in Chapter 4.
Source: pp. 75-111 (PDF: pp. 97-134).

#### Main Summary

##### 1 Introduction

Conceding to the incompatibilist that knowledge requires discriminative abilities, a compatibilist might attempt to resolve the discrimination argument by incorporating a discriminative requirement directly into the possession conditions for first-order thought. If having a thought requires possessing the ability to distinguish the object of thought from other objects, then anyone who succeeds in having the thought might automatically possess the discriminative capacity required for privileged access. Evans's anti-individualist theory of singular thought offers the prime test case for this strategy, demanding that a subject can think about an object only if she can distinguish it from all other things. Brown investigates whether this condition enables a subject to distinguish the actual situation in which she thinks about object *a* from relevant alternative situations where she lacks this thought.
Source: pp. 75-77 (PDF: pp. 97-99).

##### 2 Evans’s Account of Singular Thought

Evans (1982) develops a Fregean, anti-individualist account of singular thought governed by Russell's Principle: a subject cannot make a judgment about an object unless she knows which object her judgment is about. In contrast to pure causal theories (Kripke, Devitt, Fodor)—which Evans refutes via his "steel balls" thought experiment, where a subject with amnesia causally traces memories to a second identical rotating steel ball but can do nothing to distinguish it from the first—Evans requires that a subject be able to discriminate the referent from all other things.
Source: pp. 77-80 (PDF: pp. 99-102).

This requirement does not demand distinguishing objects by bare perceptual appearance alone. Instead, Evans identifies three legitimate ways a subject exploits environmental relations to achieve absolute discrimination:
1. *By description*: Identifying the object as uniquely satisfying an individuating condition (`the F`). (This is descriptive and not externally individuated).
2. *By perception (perceptual demonstratives)*: Currently perceiving an object enables the subject to locate it in egocentric and public space and identify its fundamental sortal type. Since a spatiotemporal object is uniquely individuated by its location and sortal type, perception provides absolute discrimination even when duplicates exist elsewhere.
3. *By recognitional capacity (recognition-based thoughts)*: Re-identifying an object encountered in the past. Because similarity of appearance does not guarantee identity, the subject must grasp spatiotemporal constraints, estimating an "area of search" centered on the previous sighting. A subject has a recognitional capacity for *a* if and only if *a* is the sole object within the estimated area of search that she is disposed to identify as the target on the basis of appearance.
Source: pp. 80-83 (PDF: pp. 102-105).

##### 3 Evans and the Discrimination Argument

Brown assesses whether Evans's conditions for singular thought resolve the discrimination argument. A fundamental limitation immediately emerges: the discrimination argument exploits any relevant alternative where the subject lacks the target thought. This includes *no-reference situations* (where the subject hallucinates an object or mistakenly amalgamates multiple objects). Evans accepts the illusion version of anti-individualism, holding that in such cases the subject suffers an "illusion of thought." However, Evans does not require that a subject be able to distinguish perceiving an object from suffering an illusion of perception. Consequently, Evans's theory offers no resource to distinguish actual thoughts from no-reference illusions.
Source: pp. 83-86 (PDF: pp. 105-108).

The remaining question is whether Evans's theory at least enables a subject to distinguish her actual thought from *twin situations* involving an alternative duplicate object.
Source: pp. 85-86 (PDF: pp. 107-108).

##### 4 Perceptual Demonstrative Thoughts

Brown demonstrates that for perceptual demonstrative thoughts, Evans's requirement fails to secure the discriminative capacity needed for privileged access. Suppose Sally sees Anya playing in the garden. By perceiving Anya, Sally locates her and identifies her sortal type, thus meeting Evans's condition for thinking "that girl (Anya) is happy." However, Anya has an identical twin, Tanya, living in the immediate neighborhood. It is a relevant alternative that Tanya might have occupied that exact spatial location. If Sally is distracted and Tanya is seamlessly substituted for Anya, Sally fails to notice the switch. After the switch, Sally's new perceptual relation allows her to think about Tanya ("that girl (Tanya) is happy"), yet she falsely judges that she is thinking the same thought as before and cannot act differentially between the two thoughts.
Source: pp. 86-90 (PDF: pp. 108-112).

Perception enables a subject to exploit her *actual* spatial relation to distinguish an object from others in the actual scene, but this provides no ability to distinguish the actual situation from a counterfactual situation where a duplicate occupies that same spatial relation.
Source: pp. 87-88 (PDF: pp. 109-110).

##### 5 Possible Compatibilist Responses

Brown defends her perceptual demonstrative argument against three potential compatibilist objections:
1. *The illusion of thought objection*: Claiming that failing to notice the switch induces an illusion of thought rather than two distinct thoughts. Brown responds that even if object-tracking over time is required for demonstrative thought, tracking over finite intervals before and after the switch suffices to establish genuine thoughts about Anya and Tanya respectively, leaving the discriminative failure intact.
Source: pp. 90-91 (PDF: pp. 112-113).
2. *The successive thought / memory objection*: Arguing that because Sally cannot entertain both thoughts simultaneously, her failure is merely a failure of memory. Brown replies that failure to notice transitions across successive states undermines knowledge of state (as in unmonitored heart arrhythmias), and that Sally's subjective phenomenal indistinguishability explains her discriminative incapacity independently of memory.
Source: pp. 91-93 (PDF: pp. 113-115).
3. *Discrimination by description*: Proposing that Sally can distinguish the situations indexically ("the thought I am actually having") or via content-ascription ("the situation in which I think that Anya is happy"). Brown refutes this by noting that indexical descriptions yield only trivial, content-blind knowledge (analogous to knowing "I am here" when lost), while content-based descriptions would absurdly grant the fake-barn observer discriminative knowledge merely by conceiving her situation as "the situation where there is a real barn."
Source: pp. 93-96 (PDF: pp. 115-118).

##### 6 Recognition-based Thoughts

Turning to recognition-based thoughts, Brown investigates whether having a recognitional capacity for object *x* prevents relevant duplicate alternatives.
Source: pp. 96-98 (PDF: pp. 118-120).

Under Evans's definition, the area of search is the *estimated* area of search—the region where the subject subjectively expects the object to be. Brown demonstrates that this formulation leads to unacceptable paradoxes through the case of Naive and Sage. Both encounter cat Tabby. Naive is ignorant of cat physiology and underestimates Tabby's speed, establishing a tiny estimated search area ($A_N$) containing only Tabby. Sage is an expert who correctly estimates Tabby's potential range ($A_S$), which contains both Tabby and an identical duplicate cat, Kitty. On Evans's definition, Naive possesses a recognitional capacity and can think about Tabby *because of her false belief*, whereas Sage is denied the capacity *because of her true knowledge*. Furthermore, since recognition yields knowledge of identity, Evans's view absurdly implies that false beliefs generate knowledge that true beliefs prevent.
Source: pp. 98-103 (PDF: pp. 120-125).

Brown resolves this by modifying Evans's account: the search area must be the *actual area of search*—the objective region within which the object could correctly be estimated to be. On this modified account, a subject has a recognitional capacity for *x* only if there is no duplicate in the *actual* area of search that she would mistake for *x*. This entails that if a subject possesses a genuine recognitional capacity for *x*, there are *no relevant duplicates* in the actual search area. Consequently, for recognition-based thoughts, the subject *can* distinguish a priori between thinking about *x* and thinking about a relevant duplicate, because the very conditions for having the thought rule out the existence of relevant look-alikes.
Source: pp. 101-106 (PDF: pp. 123-128).

##### 7 Comparison of Evans’s Account and the Modified Account

Brown compares Evans's original account with her modified account. While Evans claimed recognitional capacities are "absolutely discriminating" (ruling out all objects in the universe), Brown's modified account makes them *relatively discriminating*—ruling out duplicates within the relevant actual area of search.
Source: pp. 106-108 (PDF: pp. 128-130).

Crucially, Brown shows that this modification allows recognitional capacities to be extended to *natural kinds*. Kinds have no spatiotemporal boundaries, so no subject can have an absolutely discriminating capacity for a kind across the entire cosmos (e.g., distinguishing Earth swallows from hypothetical duplicates on distant planets). Instead, a subject has a recognitional capacity for a kind *k* if and only if she can distinguish it from all other kinds in her *local actual environment*. This relaxes Russell's Principle from an impossible cosmic demand to an epistemologically realistic relevant-alternatives condition.
Source: pp. 108-110 (PDF: pp. 130-132).

##### 8 Conclusion

Brown synthesizes the conclusions of Chapter 3. Evans's attempt to meet the discrimination requirement on knowledge by building absolute discrimination into thought fails for no-reference cases and for perceptual demonstratives. However, for recognition-based thoughts about objects and kinds, Brown's modified account achieves a vital result: having a recognitional capacity entails that there are no relevant duplicates in the subject's local environment. While this does not completely defeat the discrimination argument on its own (since no-reference illusions remain), it provides a powerful epistemological bridge to Chapter 4, where Brown utilizes the concept of relevant alternatives to defuse both the discrimination and illusion arguments.
Source: pp. 110-111 (PDF: pp. 132-134).

#### Important Concepts

- **Evans's discrimination requirement (Russell's Principle).**
  Definition: The principle that a subject can entertain a singular thought about an object only if she knows which object it is, requiring the ability to distinguish that object from all other things in the universe.
  Source: pp. 75-77, 89-91 (PDF: pp. 97-99, 111-113).

- **Perceptual demonstrative thought.**
  Definition: A singular thought about an object based on current perception, where the perceptual relation enables the subject to locate the object in space and identify its sortal type.
  Source: pp. 81-83, 86-90 (PDF: pp. 103-105, 108-112).

- **Estimated area of search vs. actual area of search.**
  Definition: The estimated area of search is the subjective area where a thinker estimates a previously seen object to be (Evans); the actual area of search is the objective area where the object could correctly be estimated to be given time elapsed, speed, and last known location (Brown).
  Source: pp. 98-106 (PDF: pp. 120-128).

- **Modified account of recognitional capacities.**
  Definition: Brown's thesis that a subject has a recognitional capacity for an object (or kind) only if it is the sole object (or kind) within the actual area of search (or local actual environment) that she is disposed to identify as the target, entailing that no relevant duplicates exist.
  Source: pp. 104-110 (PDF: pp. 126-132).

#### Formal-Logical Argument Reconstruction: Inadequacy of Evans's Discrimination Requirement for Perceptual Demonstratives
1. On Evans's account, a subject $S$ satisfies the conditions for thinking the perceptual demonstrative thought that that girl (Anya) is happy because perceiving Anya enables $S$ to determine Anya's location and sortal type, distinguishing her from all other objects in the actual environment. Source: pp. 86-88 (PDF: pp. 108-110).
2. If Anya has an identical twin Tanya in the vicinity, there is a relevant alternative situation in which Tanya occupies that exact spatial location and $S$ thinks that that girl (Tanya) is happy. Source: pp. 86-88 (PDF: pp. 108-110).
3. If Tanya replaces Anya unawares, everything seems subjectively identical to $S$, such that $S$ cannot notice the switch, cannot make reliable sameness/difference judgments, and cannot act differentially between the two thoughts. Source: pp. 88-90 (PDF: pp. 110-112).
4. If $S$ cannot notice a change, make reliable sameness/difference judgments, or act differentially between two thoughts, $S$ cannot distinguish between them a priori. Source: pp. 89-90 (PDF: pp. 111-112).
5. Therefore, satisfying Evans's discrimination requirement for having a perceptual demonstrative thought does not confer the discriminative ability required for a priori knowledge of that thought. Source: pp. 90, 110-111 (PDF: pp. 112, 132-133) (from 1-4).

### 4 The Illusion Argument

#### Section Abstract

Jessica Brown examines the illusion argument—an incompatibilist challenge specifically targeting the illusion version of anti-individualism (Evans, McDowell, Boghossian). In no-reference situations (hallucinations, Dry Earth, Motley Earth), subjects fail to entertain any thought and suffer an "illusion of thought," raising the worry that thinkers lack the reliability necessary for privileged access. Brown shows that while self-verifying cogito thoughts maintain local reliability, they face serious threats from global reliability requirements via the generality problem. To resolve both the illusion argument and the discrimination argument without taking sides in the stalemate over whether knowledge requires discriminative ability or merely reliability, Brown develops the *relevance strategy*. She systematically demonstrates that counterfactual twin and no-reference situations are not normally relevant alternatives: (1) for social anti-individualism, normal linguistic conditions rule out widespread ignorant slow switching; (2) for natural kind and recognition-based thoughts, the possession of recognitional capacities entails the absence of relevant duplicates; and (3) for no-reference illusions, pervasive error is ruled out on pain of radical perceptual skepticism. Except for certain dense duplicate environments in perceptual demonstratives, this relevant-alternatives framework provides a comprehensive defense of privileged access.
Source: pp. 113-156 (PDF: pp. 137-180).

#### Main Summary

##### 1 Introduction

While the discrimination argument applies to all forms of externalism, the *illusion argument* specifically challenges the illusion version of anti-individualism. On this view, in no-reference situations—where an intended object or kind is absent (such as perceptual hallucinations, Boghossian's "Dry Earth" where water is an illusion, or "Motley Earth" where apparent water is an unstable mixture)—the subject fails to express any determinate thought content, suffering an "illusion of thought." If a subject frequently suffers illusions of thought (e.g., Sally, sensitized by a nearby nest, frequently hallucinates hearing a wasp), she is prone to radical second-order error: taking herself to have a demonstrative thought ("that wasp is near") when she has none. The incompatibilist argues that this unreliability undermines a priori knowledge of thought contents in veridical cases.
Source: pp. 113-117 (PDF: pp. 137-141).

##### 2 Relevance and Reliability

Compatibilists standardly attempt to answer the illusion argument by conceding the relevance of the illusion alternative while defending the reliability of self-verifying cogito thoughts. When Sally hallucinates, she lacks the object-dependent concept of wasp $w$; hence, she cannot falsely self-ascribe the thought *that wasp (w) is near*. In the hallucination, she merely self-ascribes whatever thoughts she actually has (e.g., descriptive thoughts like *the wasp I seem to hear is near*). However, the incompatibilist retorts that Sally still makes a profound second-order mistake: she falsely believes she is entertaining a perceptual demonstrative thought about a perceived wasp. This mistake concerns the fundamental nature of her mental state, not mere external relations.
Source: pp. 117-119 (PDF: pp. 141-143).

##### 3 Two Notions of Reliability

To evaluate whether this second-order error undermines knowledge, Brown distinguishes two epistemological conceptions of reliability:
- *Local reliability*: A belief-forming process is locally reliable if and only if it would not easily produce a false belief in that specific proposition $p$ (Nozick's tracking conditions; Goldman's 1986 counterfactual test).
- *Global reliability*: A belief-forming process is globally reliable if and only if it is a process-type that tends to produce a high ratio of true beliefs across a broader range of propositions of the same level and subject matter (McGinn 1984; Goldman 1986).
Brown shows that Sally's self-verifying cogito thoughts are locally reliable: in the hallucination world where she does not think *that wasp (w) is near*, the process does not produce the belief that she thinks *that wasp (w) is near*. If knowledge requires only local reliability, compatibilism is vindicated.
Source: pp. 119-122 (PDF: pp. 143-146).

##### 4 Local or Global Reliability?

Brown demonstrates that local reliability is insufficient for knowledge, establishing that global reliability is a mandatory condition. First, in Gettier cases involving necessary truths (e.g., accepting mathematical truth from a deceitful informant who happens to tell the truth), local reliability is trivially satisfied because the proposition cannot be false in any possible world, yet the belief is not knowledge. Second, in singular perceptual demonstratives in fake-barn country, Laura perceives a real barn $b$ and believes "that object ($b$) is a barn." Even if barn $b$ is essentially a barn (making it locally impossible for $b$ not to be a barn while believed to be one), Laura lacks knowledge because the process fails over the broader class of propositions of the form "$x$ is a barn." Thus, knowledge requires global reliability.
Source: pp. 122-125 (PDF: pp. 146-149).

##### 5 The Illusion Argument Reconsidered

Because knowledge demands global reliability, Sally's self-knowledge requires that her belief-forming process be reliable across relevant second-order thoughts. If the relevant process is typed broadly as "forming beliefs about whether one is having a perceptual demonstrative thought," Sally is globally unreliable due to frequent wasp hallucinations. Compatibilists might attempt to restrict the process to "forming self-verifying cogito thoughts," but this encounters the notorious *generality problem*: there is no principled, non-ad-hoc justification for privileging the narrow cogito typing over the broader mental-state typing. Thus, appealing to reliabilism cannot cleanly defeat the illusion argument.
Source: pp. 125-129 (PDF: pp. 149-153).

##### 6 Conclusion of the Illusion Argument

Brown concludes that defending the illusion version of anti-individualism by conceding relevance and asserting reliability fails. If global reliability is necessary for knowledge, frequent illusions of thought jeopardize privileged access. A successful defense must challenge the *relevance* of the illusion alternative itself.
Source: pp. 129-130 (PDF: pp. 153-154).

##### 7 The Illusion and Discrimination Arguments

Synthesizing the lessons of Chapters 2 through 4, Brown proposes a unified compatibilist breakthrough: the *relevance strategy*. Instead of debating whether knowledge requires discriminative abilities or mere reliability, the compatibilist should show that counterfactual twin and no-reference situations are *not normally relevant alternatives*. If these alternatives are irrelevant, they cannot undermine knowledge under either reliabilist or discriminative frameworks, resolving both the discrimination and illusion arguments simultaneously.
Source: pp. 130-132 (PDF: pp. 154-156).

##### 8 Social Anti-Individualism and Twin Situations

Brown demonstrates that for social anti-individualism, twin situations are not normally relevant. A slow-switch scenario where linguistic variation undermines knowledge requires three joint conditions: (1) two communities share a word-form with slightly different definitions; (2) the speaker is ignorant of the difference; and (3) the speaker is a fully competent user in both communities. Brown proves that these conditions cannot normally be met across a population. If slow switching were pervasive (e.g., citizens routinely alternating between the UK and US), linguistic divergence would rapidly collapse into a unified dialect, or speakers would become conscious of the differences, eliminating ignorance. Thus, social twin alternatives are inherently abnormal and irrelevant.
Source: pp. 132-135 (PDF: pp. 156-159).

##### 9 Natural Kind Anti-Individualism and Twin Situations

For natural kind anti-individualism, Twin Earth scenarios involving "twater" (XYZ) are pure science fiction and irrelevant to ordinary Earth thinkers. Furthermore, for genuine recognition-based kind concepts (such as recognizing gold or water), Brown applies her modified account of recognitional capacities: possessing a recognitional capacity for kind $k$ entails that there are no relevant look-alike duplicate kinds in the subject's local actual environment. Consequently, no relevant twin-kind alternative exists.
Source: pp. 135-138 (PDF: pp. 159-162).

##### 10 Singular Anti-Individualism and Twin Situations

For singular thoughts:
- *Recognition-based singular thoughts*: By definition, possessing a recognitional capacity for object $x$ requires that no duplicate exists within the actual area of search. Hence, twin alternatives are excluded as irrelevant.
- *Perceptual demonstrative thoughts*: In many ordinary contexts (e.g., looking at an ancient beech tree or an acquaintance without an identical twin), twin alternatives are completely irrelevant. However, in dense duplicate environments (e.g., sorting identical apples on a conveyor belt, or observing seagulls in a flock), twin alternatives *are* relevant, and the subject lacks a priori self-knowledge of the exact singular content. Brown concedes this bounded exception, noting that partial vulnerability in perceptual demonstratives does not threaten the general validity of privileged access.
Source: pp. 138-141 (PDF: pp. 162-165).

##### 11 No-Reference Situations

Brown shows that no-reference situations (illusions of perception and category confusions) are not normally relevant. Perceptual hallucinations cannot be pervasive, because if they were, empirical knowledge of the external world would be impossible: subjects would require independent non-perceptual justification to rule out hallucination, generating an inescapable skeptical regress. Because perceptual experience normally yields knowledge of the world, perceptual illusions are not normally relevant alternatives; *a fortiori*, illusions of thought based on perception are not normally relevant. Similarly, confusing multiple items into an amalgamated kind is ruled out by the conditions for recognitional capacities and successful object-tracking.
Source: pp. 141-146 (PDF: pp. 165-170).

##### 12 The Relevance Strategy and Alternatives

Brown defends the relevance strategy against two objections:
1. *The accidentality objection*: The objection that making privileged access depend on environmental stability makes self-knowledge a fragile accident. Brown refutes this: the absence of relevant alternatives is guaranteed by the metaphysical conditions for recognitional capacities, the sociolinguistic stability of communities, and the preconditions of perceptual knowledge.
2. *The perceptual demonstrative limitation*: The objection that failing to cover dense duplicate environments weakens compatibilism. Brown counters that the strategy successfully protects social, natural kind, recognition-based, and most singular demonstratives, providing a robust two-pronged defense: where alternatives are irrelevant, they do not undermine knowledge; where they are relevant, self-verifying cogito reliability preserves truth.
Source: pp. 146-149 (PDF: pp. 170-173).

##### 13 Conclusion of Chapters 2 through 4

Brown concludes Part I of the monograph. The achievement problem is solved: anti-individualism is fully compatible with privileged access. Having defused the threat to self-knowledge, Brown announces that the subsequent parts of the book (reasoning, Fregean sense, and the reductio) proceed independently of the debate between discrimination and reliability, requiring only minimal, uncontroversial epistemic assumptions.
Source: pp. 149-150 (PDF: pp. 173-174).

#### Important Concepts

- **Illusion argument.**
  Definition: An incompatibilist argument maintaining that on the illusion version of anti-individualism, the possibility of suffering an illusion of thought in no-reference situations undermines the global reliability required for a priori self-knowledge of thought contents.
  Source: pp. 113-117, 134-135 (PDF: pp. 137-141, 158-159).

- **Local reliability vs. global reliability.**
  Definition: Local reliability requires that a belief-forming process would not easily produce a false belief in that specific proposition $p$; global reliability requires that the process-type tends to produce a high ratio of true beliefs over a wider range of propositions of the same kind.
  Source: pp. 119-124, 129-133 (PDF: pp. 143-148, 153-157).

- **Relevant alternatives strategy.**
  Definition: Brown's compatibilist defense establishing that counterfactual twin and no-reference situations are not normally relevant alternatives, thereby safeguarding privileged access without needing to resolve whether knowledge requires discriminative abilities or mere reliability.
  Source: pp. 135-138, 152-155 (PDF: pp. 159-162, 176-179).

#### Formal-Logical Argument Reconstruction: The Relevance Defense of Privileged Access
1. A counterfactual situation in which a subject $S$ lacks the thought that $p$ undermines $S$'s a priori knowledge that she thinks that $p$ only if that counterfactual situation is a relevant alternative. Source: pp. 117-119, 135-138 (PDF: pp. 141-143, 159-162).
2. For socially individuated thoughts, twin situations require conditions (widespread ignorant slow-switching across dialects) that cannot normally obtain without collapsing dialectical differences. Source: pp. 138-142 (PDF: pp. 162-166).
3. For natural kind thoughts and recognition-based singular thoughts, possessing a recognitional capacity entails by definition that there are no relevant duplicate kinds or objects in the actual search area/environment. Source: pp. 142-146 (PDF: pp. 166-170).
4. For all thoughts, no-reference (perceptual illusion or Dry/Motley Earth) situations are not normally relevant, on pain of global perceptual skepticism. Source: pp. 147-152 (PDF: pp. 171-176).
5. Therefore, for socially individuated, natural kind, and recognition-based thoughts (and non-duplicate perceptual demonstratives), alternative situations are not normally relevant, preserving privileged access. Source: pp. 152-156 (PDF: pp. 176-180) (from 1-4).


### 5 Anti-Individualism and Reasoning

#### Section Abstract
Jessica Brown evaluates the objection that anti-individualism undermines a thinker's ability to reason by destroying the a priori transparency of content. She distinguishes between the transparency of sameness of content (knowing a priori when two simultaneously entertained thoughts share content) and the transparency of difference of content (knowing a priori when they differ). Non-Fregean anti-individualism fails transparency of sameness because a thinker can partially understand co-referential terms (e.g., coriander and cilantro) without knowing they express the same concept, though Fregean anti-individualism avoids this by distinguishing senses based on cognitive significance. However, both Fregean and non-Fregean anti-individualism fail transparency of difference in slow-switch scenarios: switched thinkers retain past concepts alongside new environmental concepts under a single term, leading to simple invalid inferences through conceptual equivocation. Brown refutes Burge's preservative memory defense and Campbell-style identity-premise models, demonstrating that thinkers must rely directly on conceptual identity. Crucially, Brown defends anti-individualism against Boghossian's charge that this destroys rationality: it merely refutes an idealized conception of rationality as a priori conformity to logic ("B-rationality"), which is independently falsified by empirical psychology and philosophical puzzles. Psychological explanations of action remain intact, and externalism provides a principled criterion for blameless logical error.

#### Main Summary

##### 1 Introduction
Brown introduces the reasoning objection against anti-individualism: if mental contents are individuated by environmental relations of which a thinker may be ignorant, a subject cannot know a priori whether two simultaneously entertained thoughts have the same or different content. Because sameness and difference of content govern logical validity and consistency, externalism appears to imply that subjects cannot discern the logical properties of their thoughts without empirical investigation of the external world, thereby threatening our fundamental conception of thinkers as rational agents. Brown sharpens the debate by setting aside diachronic belief revision—where changing beliefs over time is not irrational—and focusing strictly on synchronic rationality. She formalizes two distinct epistemic principles:
1. *Transparency of sameness of content*: For any two thoughts or thought constituents entertained by a subject $S$ at time $t$, if they have the same content, $S$ can realize a priori at $t$ that they have the same content.
2. *Transparency of difference of content*: For any two thoughts or thought constituents entertained by a subject $S$ at time $t$, if they have different contents, $S$ can realize a priori at $t$ that they have different contents.
Brown's strategy is twofold: first, examine the extent to which standard anti-individualist views undermine these principles; second, evaluate whether the failure of transparency genuinely poses a fatal objection to anti-individualism and rationality.
Source: pp. 158-161 (PDF: pp. 184-187).

##### 2 Transparency of Sameness of Content
Brown reviews the established arguments (Owens, Boghossian, Millikan, Falvey and Owens) showing that non-Fregean anti-individualism is incompatible with the transparency of sameness. In Falvey and Owens's classic case, Rudolf partially understands 'coriander' (known as a dried spice) and 'cilantro' (known as a fresh Mexican herb), deferring to experts for their explication. Because both terms refer to the identical plant kind, non-Fregean externalists must hold that Rudolf expresses the same concept by both terms. Yet because Rudolf is ignorant of their co-reference, he cannot realize a priori that his beliefs ('Coriander grows in Britain' and 'Cilantro grows in France') entail that something grows in both countries, nor can he realize a priori that believing cilantro was known to ancient Romans while coriander was not constitutes an inconsistency. Brown extends this failure to social anti-individualism using Segal's case of Alf, who mistakenly believes 'optician' has two distinct meanings (a general optics expert vs. an eye-wear dispenser); since Alf defers to experts whose single term expresses one concept, Alf unwittingly expresses the identical concept in both uses, violating transparency of sameness. However, Brown establishes a crucial asymmetry: this failure applies only to non-Fregean views. Fregean anti-individualists individuate sense by cognitive significance (Evans, Campbell, Peacocke): because it is rationally possible for Rudolf to assent to one claim while dissenting from the other, he associates different senses with the two terms. For the Fregean, the thoughts have different contents; hence Rudolf neither contradicts himself nor fails to perform a valid inference.
Source: pp. 161-166 (PDF: pp. 187-192).

##### 3 Transparency of Difference of Content
Turning to the transparency of difference, Brown examines slow-switch cases (Boghossian, Falvey and Owens, Goldberg). Suppose Sally is unwittingly transported from Earth to Twin Earth, where 'aluminum' refers to twaluminum. Before the switch, she forms the belief (1) 'Aluminum is mined in Australia' (involving the Earth concept *aluminum*). After extended interaction on Twin Earth, she forms the belief (2) 'Aluminum is mined in the Soviet Union' (involving the Twin Earth concept *twaluminum*). If Sally retains her memory-based Earth belief while acquiring the Twin Earth belief, she simultaneously holds beliefs involving two distinct concepts under the identical linguistic token 'aluminum'. Consequently, when Sally infers (3) 'Something is mined in both Australia and the Soviet Union', her inference commits a fallacy of four terms (equivocation) and is invalid. Because Sally cannot know without empirical investigation that her premises involve different concepts, she cannot determine the logical validity of her inference a priori. Crucially, Brown demonstrates that unlike transparency of sameness, the failure of transparency of difference strikes *both* non-Fregean and Fregean anti-individualism. Because *aluminum* and *twaluminum* differ in extension in the external world, they necessarily differ in content for any externalist, and Fregean sense cannot unify thoughts whose external referents diverge.
Source: pp. 166-170 (PDF: pp. 192-196).

##### 4 Slow Switch and Conceptual Change
Brown evaluates attempts by anti-individualists to resist the two-concept view of slow switching. One strategy denies that a switcher retains two concepts under a single term, arguing that the subject's concept changes completely or forms an amalgam:
1. *Tye's recollection-environment account*: Tye claims memory content is determined by the environment at the time of recollection, so an Earth switcher reminiscing on Twin Earth uses only *twater*, misremembering his youth. Brown refutes Tye by revealing that his argument presupposes the very transparency of difference in dispute: Tye infers sameness of concept from the subject's belief that he is comparing the same liquid, which begs the question against failure of transparency.
2. *Heal's exemplar account*: Heal argues a concept's extension is fixed by all exemplars a subject currently regards as standard-setting (including past Earth memories and present Twin Earth encounters), yielding a unified "amalgam" concept ($H_2O \lor XYZ$). Brown notes this relies on an idiosyncratic, dispositional theory of content rejected by mainstream historical and causal theorists.
3. *Historical-causal accounts and Burge's preservative memory*: Causal theorists can localize content to distinct causal chains, sustaining two concepts. Furthermore, Burge provides independent justification for the two-concept view: the primary epistemic function of preservative memory is to preserve past contents and make them available for later thought without converting them into empirical premises about past acts of remembering. Thus, anti-individualists have strong, principled reasons to accept the two-concept model.
Source: pp. 170-176 (PDF: pp. 196-202).

##### 5 Two Concepts and Inference
Brown investigates whether an anti-individualist who accepts the two-concept view can prevent the slow switcher from making invalid inferences. She refutes the two leading proposals:
1. *Burge's preservative memory constraint*: Burge claims that preservative memory functions to hold constant the concept introduced in the initial premise throughout an inferential chain, forcing premise (2) to adopt the concept of premise (1). Brown refutes this by showing it produces an absurd, order-dependent metaphysics of thought content: if Sally begins with the Earth premise, her second thought is about aluminum; but if she begins with the Twin Earth premise, her second thought is about twaluminum. This denies that thought contents are independent of the arbitrary sequence of reasoning and destroys the point of memory, which is to allow past thoughts to be deployed freely in inference.
2. *The implicit identity-premise hypothesis*: It might be argued that Sally's inference is valid but unsound because it contains an implicit identity premise ('Aluminum is aluminum'). Brown refutes this by applying John Campbell's regress argument. If relying on the identity of concepts across premises always required an explicit identity premise, even an ordinary non-switched thinker inferring from 'Water is clear' and 'Water is good to drink' would require an identity premise ('Water is water'); but that premise itself requires identity between its own occurrences and the prior premises, launching an infinite regress. Nor can the regress be halted by higher-order premises ('there is no conceptual equivocation'), which absurdly require ground-floor reasoners to possess the meta-concept of a concept. Therefore, rational thinkers must directly rely on the identity of concepts, which entails that when environmental switches occur unawares, thinkers inevitably make invalid inferences through equivocation.
Source: pp. 176-182 (PDF: pp. 202-208).

##### 6 Rationality and A Priority
Brown addresses the core philosophical threat: does the failure of transparency destroy rationality? Boghossian argues that rationality consists in "B-rationality"—the ability and disposition to make one's thoughts conform to the laws of logic strictly on an a priori basis, accessible even to an envatted brain. Brown delivers a multi-stage defense of anti-individualism:
1. *Distinguishing logical competence from content identification*: Following Campbell, Brown distinguishes the ability to grasp a priori that an argument of a certain form is valid (formal logical competence) from the ability to know a priori which logical form one's thoughts instantiate. Externalism leaves pure logical competence intact while showing that identifying the form of one's thoughts requires environmental facts.
2. *Independent failure of B-rationality*: Brown shows that ordinary human beings are not B-rational regardless of anti-individualism. Empirical psychology (Wason selection tasks, Kahneman and Tversky's heuristics and biases) proves that subjects systematically violate simple logical rules. Furthermore, philosophical disagreements over synonymy—such as Mates's puzzle where Kripke/Church hold that 'Mary is a physician' and 'Mary is a doctor' express the identical proposition while Burge/Mates hold they express different propositions—demonstrate that highly rational thinkers can disagree persistently about whether two thoughts share content, an error resolvable only empirically.
3. *Integrity of psychological explanation*: Folk psychology relies on generalizations connecting belief, desire, and action (e.g., Fodor, Churchland). Because slow switching and unrecognized synonymies are rare anomalies, these psychological laws remain overwhelmingly reliable for predicting and explaining behavior.
4. *Blameless logical error*: Brown provides an anti-individualist criterion distinguishing blameless logical mistakes from culpable irrationality. A subject is blameless for holding contradictory beliefs ($a$ is $F$ and $a$ is not $F$) if, were the question raised, she would doubt or deny that the terms refer to the same item (Rudolf). Similarly, a subject is blameless for an invalid inference through equivocation if she would affirm the identity of the concepts were she asked (Sally). Blameworthy irrationality occurs only when an agent violates logic while acknowledging conceptual identity.
Source: pp. 182-191 (PDF: pp. 208-217).

##### 7 Conclusion
Brown summarizes the conclusions of Chapter 5. Non-Fregean anti-individualism undermines the transparency of sameness, while all forms of anti-individualism (Fregean and non-Fregean alike) undermine the transparency of difference. Consequently, thinkers cannot always grasp the logical properties of their thoughts a priori, occasionally falling into unrecognized inconsistencies or invalid equivocations. However, this constitutes no reductio of anti-individualism: it merely dismantles the ungrounded dogma of B-rationality, aligns philosophy of mind with empirical cognitive science, preserves the predictive power of psychological explanation, and provides a principled account of blameless rational agency.
Source: pp. 191-193 (PDF: pp. 217-219).

#### Important Concepts

- **Transparency of sameness of content.**
  Definition: The epistemic thesis that for any two thoughts or thought constituents entertained by a subject $S$ at time $t$, if they have the same content, $S$ can know or realize a priori at $t$ that they have the same content.
  Source: pp. 159-161, 166-167 (PDF: pp. 185-187, 192-193).

- **Transparency of difference of content.**
  Definition: The epistemic thesis that for any two thoughts or thought constituents entertained by a subject $S$ at time $t$, if they have different contents, $S$ can know or realize a priori at $t$ that they have different contents.
  Source: pp. 160-161, 167-170 (PDF: pp. 186-187, 193-196).

- **Preservative memory.**
  Definition: Tyler Burge's account of memory as a capacity whose primary epistemic function is to preserve past thought contents and their justificational force over time without introducing empirical premises about past acts of remembering into an inference.
  Source: pp. 175-176, 177-179 (PDF: pp. 201-202, 203-205).

- **B-rationality.**
  Definition: Boghossian's idealized conception of rationality as consisting in the inherent ability and disposition of a subject to conform her thoughts to the principles of logic strictly on an a priori basis, independent of any empirical facts about the external environment.
  Source: pp. 183-186 (PDF: pp. 209-212).

- **Blameless logical error.**
  Definition: Brown's normative epistemic criterion wherein a thinker is rationally blameless for logical mistakes (such as holding contradictory beliefs or equivocating across premises) if, were the question of identity explicitly raised, she would doubt/deny the sameness of referents or sincerely affirm the putative identity of distinct concepts.
  Source: pp. 189-191 (PDF: pp. 215-217).

#### Formal-Logical Argument Reconstruction: The Failure of Transparency and the Defense of Rationality
1. If anti-individualism is true, a subject's thoughts are individuated by environmental relations of which she may be ignorant. Source: pp. 158-161 (PDF: pp. 184-187).
2. If thoughts are individuated by environmental relations of which a subject may be ignorant, then in slow-switch scenarios a subject can simultaneously hold memory-based thoughts individuated by her past environment and new thoughts individuated by her present environment under the same term. Source: pp. 167-170, 175-176 (PDF: pp. 193-196, 201-202).
3. If a subject simultaneously holds thoughts with different environmental contents under the same term without empirical information about the switch, she cannot know a priori that they have different contents, and her inferences linking them are invalid through equivocation. Source: pp. 168-170, 177-182 (PDF: pp. 194-196, 203-208).
4. Therefore, anti-individualism entails that the transparency of difference of content fails, and subjects cannot grasp all logical properties of their thoughts a priori. Source: pp. 182-183 (PDF: pp. 208-209) (from 1-3).
5. An inability to grasp the logical properties of one's thoughts a priori undermines rationality only if rationality consists in B-rationality (the disposition to conform thoughts to logic on an a priori basis). Source: pp. 183-185 (PDF: pp. 209-211).
6. Human subjects are not B-rational, as demonstrated by empirical psychology (reasoning fallacies) and philosophical synonymy puzzles, and folk-psychological explanation requires only general conformity to logic coupled with a criterion of blameless error. Source: pp. 185-191 (PDF: pp. 211-217).
7. Therefore, the failure of transparency does not undermine human rationality or provide a reductio of anti-individualism. Source: pp. 191-193 (PDF: pp. 217-219) (from 4-6).


### 6 Anti-Individualism and Fregean Sense

#### Section Abstract
Jessica Brown investigates whether anti-individualism is compatible with the Fregean notion of sense. Prominent neo-Fregeans (Evans, McDowell, Peacocke, Campbell) attempt to synthesize both traditions by replacing descriptive senses with object-dependent senses (ways of thinking unavailable in the absence of the object). Brown demonstrates an intractable philosophical tension between these two commitments. While Fregean anti-individualism successfully preserves the transparency of sameness of content by individuating senses according to cognitive significance, it inevitably undermines the transparency of difference of content in both slow-switch scenarios and non-switch singular/natural kind cases (Bryan Frances's element case and Evans-style perceptual demonstratives). Although affirming transparency of sameness while denying transparency of difference is formally logically consistent, it creates an unmotivated, ad hoc epistemic asymmetry regarding rational agency: it asserts that a rational thinker can never commit simple contradictions or fail to draw simple valid inferences, yet allows that the very same rational thinker can commit simple invalid inferences through unrecognized equivocation. Non-Fregean anti-individualism avoids this incoherence through a symmetric rejection of transparency, forcing theorists to choose between externalism and Fregean psychological explanation.

#### Main Summary

##### 1 Introduction
Brown addresses the prominent philosophical project of combining anti-individualism with the Fregean notion of sense, endorsed by theorists such as Gareth Evans, John Campbell, John McDowell, and Christopher Peacocke. Fregeans maintain that the semantic value of thought constituents cannot be exhausted by external referents; sense is required to explain cognitive informativeness, inferential transitions, and propositional attitude ascriptions, while preventing the attribution of contradictory beliefs to rational thinkers. Although classic descriptive theories of sense (Kripke, Salmon) conflict with anti-individualism by making thought contents independent of external objects, neo-Fregeans circumvent this by formulating *object-dependent senses*—modes of presentation that cannot be entertained if the referent does not exist. Brown introduces a novel objection showing that even object-dependent Fregeanism remains in severe tension with anti-individualism. The argument operates via transparency of content: whereas non-Fregean anti-individualism rejects both transparency of sameness and difference, Fregean anti-individualism affirms transparency of sameness while being forced by externalism to reject transparency of difference. This generates an unmotivated epistemic asymmetry in its theory of rationality.
Source: pp. 193-195 (PDF: pp. 221-223).

##### 2 The Contrast between Fregean and Non-Fregean Anti-Individualism
Brown details how Fregeans and non-Fregeans explain core psychological phenomena using the classic case of Celeste, who unwittingly uses 'Phosphorus' (the morning star) and 'Hesperus' (the evening star) for the planet Venus:
1. *Informative identity*: 'Phosphorus is Hesperus' is an informative empirical discovery, whereas 'Phosphorus is Phosphorus' is trivial. The Fregean explains this by a difference in sense; the non-Fregean must deny that the thoughts differ in semantic information, explaining informativeness away as the cognitive impact of learning that two distinct terms share reference.
2. *Inferential transitions*: Celeste believes Hesperus is visible in the evening and that observations of Phosphorus are vital, yet fails to infer that she should observe Hesperus in the evening. For the Fregean, the inference is invalid because the premises contain different senses; for the non-Fregean, the inference is valid, but Celeste's failure does not breach rationality because she cannot know a priori that the constituents co-refer.
3. *Contradictory beliefs*: Celeste assents to 'Phosphorus rises in the morning' and 'It is not the case that Hesperus rises in the morning'. The Fregean avoids attributing contradictory beliefs because the propositions involve distinct senses; the non-Fregean accepts that Celeste believes a proposition and its negation, but insists this is blameless because sameness of content is not transparent.
4. *Attitude ascriptions*: Celeste believes that Phosphorus rises in the morning, but lacks the belief that Hesperus rises in the morning. Fregeans explain opacity by sense; non-Fregeans invoke conversational guises or pragmatic implicatures.
Brown shows that the Fregean arguments for sense depend entirely on two foundational premises: the transparency of sameness of content and an idealized conception of rationality wherein rational thinkers never hold simple contradictory beliefs or overlook simple valid inferences.
Source: pp. 195-204 (PDF: pp. 223-232).

##### 3 Fregean Anti-Individualism and Transparency of Sameness
Brown confirms that Fregean anti-individualism is fully compatible with the transparency of sameness of content. In Falvey and Owens's case, Rudolf believes 'Cilantro should be used sparingly' and 'Coriander should be used sparingly'. Whereas non-Fregeans must hold that Rudolf has two thoughts with identical content without realizing it, Fregeans individuate senses by cognitive significance (potential difference of attitude): since Rudolf can rationally assent to one while dissenting from the other, he associates different senses with the terms. Thus, the thoughts have different contents, and transparency of sameness is preserved. Brown then evaluates John Campbell's apparent argument that Fregean object-dependent thought undermines "transparency of sense." Campbell examines a subject tracking a glass across an unnoticed switch, inferring from 'That glass is $F$' and 'That glass is $G$' that something is $F$ and $G$. Campbell claims that when tracking fails, the subject mistakenly thinks she thinks with a single sense. Brown shows this does not undermine transparency of sameness: the subject does not possess two thoughts with identical content that she mistakenly thinks differ; rather, she either suffers an illusion of thought or possesses thoughts about different objects. Campbell affirms that whenever thoughts genuinely share sense, their identity and the validity of the inference are immediately obvious to the thinker.
Source: pp. 204-210 (PDF: pp. 232-238).

##### 4 Fregean Anti-Individualism and Transparency of Difference
Brown proves that Fregean anti-individualism is inescapably incompatible with the transparency of difference of content. When externalist conditions differ, thoughts necessarily differ in content, and Fregean sense cannot unify thoughts whose referents diverge. Brown extends this incompatibilist result to singular perceptual demonstrative thoughts:
- *The Eve apple-switch case*: Eve views apple $a$, thinking (1) 'That apple is green', and after an unnoticed substitution views apple $b$, thinking (2) 'That apple is from New Zealand', inferring (3) 'Something is green and from New Zealand'. If Eve has distinct thoughts about $a$ and $b$, she cannot know a priori that they differ in content, and her inference is invalid through equivocation.
- *Tracking and discrimination requirements*: Neo-Fregeans might object that Eve suffers an illusion of thought rather than thinking two thoughts. Brown refutes this by modifying the scenario: Eve tracks apple $a$ across an extended period (establishing a stable demonstrative memory-based thought about $a$) and subsequently tracks apple $b$ across an extended period (establishing a perceptual demonstrative thought about $b$). While Gareth Evans's strict discrimination requirement (requiring knowledge of an object's spatial location to have an "adequate Idea") would deny that Eve can think about $a$ once switched, Brown points out that Evans's requirement is rejected by historical-causal externalists and most neo-Fregeans (Peacocke, Brewer). Thus, Fregean singular thought fails transparency of difference.
Source: pp. 210-217 (PDF: pp. 238-245).

##### 4.1 Frances’s Einsteinium/Fermium Case
To establish that the failure of transparency of difference is not limited to slow-switch or two-concept scenarios, Brown analyzes Bryan Frances's non-switching case:
- *The scenario*: Clara knows 'Einsteinium' and 'Fermium' name chemical elements, but is ignorant of their properties and wonders whether they might be alternative names for the same element. Because the elements are distinct, Clara's thoughts have different contents; yet she cannot determine by introspective reflection alone that they differ in content, requiring empirical chemistry to discover their difference.
- *Universal applicability*: Frances's case avoids all debates about whether slow-switch thinkers possess one concept or an amalgam (Heal). Clara maintains distinct deferential and causal chains to expert linguistic communities for each term, ensuring distinct concepts under any externalist theory.
- *Rebuttal of Fregean defenses*:
  1. *The implicit non-Fregean assumption*: Brown shows the argument does not require non-Fregeanism to be true; it requires only that Clara can entertain the possibility that the terms co-refer, which prevents her from knowing a priori that they differ.
  2. *A priori derivation of Fregean theory*: It might be argued that Clara could discover a priori through philosophical reflection that her uncertainty allows potential difference of attitude, thereby deducing that the thoughts differ in sense. Brown refutes this as absurd: one cannot claim an agent possesses a priori knowledge in virtue of a sophisticated philosophical theory (Fregean semantics) of which she is currently ignorant or unconvinced, just as an untrained person cannot solve mathematical problems merely because she could solve them were she to invent calculus or trigonometry.
- *Perceptual demonstrative application*: Brown applies Frances's argument to Evans-style perceptual demonstratives: Sally watches a symphony orchestra, seeing one clarinetist ('That clarinetist is beautiful') and hearing a distinct clarinetist solo ('That clarinetist is musical'), wondering whether they are identical. Both thoughts satisfy Evans's locating requirement, yet Sally cannot know a priori that their contents differ without empirical investigation (e.g., watching hand movements).
Source: pp. 217-226 (PDF: pp. 245-254).

##### 5 Fregean Anti-Individualism
Brown evaluates the overall coherence of Fregean anti-individualism. The position generates three stark asymmetries:
1. *Epistemic asymmetry of transparency*: *Qua* Fregean, the theorist affirms transparency of sameness; *qua* anti-individualist, she denies transparency of difference.
2. *Asymmetry of logical grasp*: The Fregean anti-individualist insists that a rational thinker can always grasp simple instances of validity and contradiction a priori, yet admits that a rational thinker cannot grasp simple instances of invalidity a priori.
3. *Asymmetry of rational error*: The position maintains that a rational thinker can never commit simple contradictions or fail to draw simple valid inferences, but readily allows that a rational thinker can commit simple invalid inferences (equivocations).
Brown concedes that affirming transparency of sameness while denying transparency of difference is formally logically consistent (analogous to red-green color blindness, where mistaking distinct colors for identical does not imply mistaking identical colors for distinct). However, she argues that the position is fundamentally *unmotivated*: what principled conception of rationality could explain why a thinker is cognitively immune to simple contradictions yet helpless before simple invalidities? Non-Fregean anti-individualism is systematically symmetric: it rejects transparency of both sameness and difference, recognizing that external ignorance can generate blameless errors of both valid omission and invalid commission. Brown concludes that theorists face an unavoidable dilemma: either embrace non-Fregean externalism and abandon idealized rationality, or retain Fregean psychological explanation and abandon anti-individualism.
Source: pp. 226-231 (PDF: pp. 254-259).

#### Important Concepts

- **Object-dependent sense.**
  Definition: A Fregean mode of presentation (developed by Evans, McDowell, and Peacocke) that is inherently individuated by the existence and identity of the external object it presents, such that in the absence of that object, no such sense is available to be entertained.
  Source: pp. 194-195, 206-207 (PDF: pp. 222-223, 234-235).

- **Potential difference of attitude.**
  Definition: The core Fregean criterion for the individuation of sense, holding that if it is rationally possible for a subject to assent to $p$ while withholding assent from or denying $q$, the contents $p$ and $q$ necessarily differ in sense.
  Source: pp. 195-196, 205-206 (PDF: pp. 223-224, 233-234).

- **Frances's element case.**
  Definition: Bryan Frances's counterexample to the transparency of difference of content, in which a thinker holds beliefs about two distinct natural kinds (e.g., Einsteinium and Fermium) with separate causal-deferential histories, yet cannot discover a priori that the thoughts differ in content because she is empirically unsure whether the terms co-refer.
  Source: pp. 217-221 (PDF: pp. 245-249).

- **Asymmetry of transparency in Fregean anti-individualism.**
  Definition: The theoretical tension wherein Fregean anti-individualism affirms the transparency of sameness of content (via cognitive significance) while denying the transparency of difference of content (via externalist individuation), resulting in an unmotivated division in its model of rational competence.
  Source: pp. 226-230 (PDF: pp. 254-258).

#### Formal-Logical Argument Reconstruction: The Tension between Anti-Individualism and Fregean Sense
1. The classic arguments for Fregean sense (explaining informative identity, inferential transitions, and non-contradiction) depend on the transparency of sameness of content and an idealized conception of rationality where agents grasp validity and avoid contradictions a priori. Source: pp. 195-204 (PDF: pp. 223-232).
2. Anti-individualism entails that thoughts are individuated by external environments, which makes thoughts about different external objects or kinds necessarily differ in content. Source: pp. 210-213, 217-221 (PDF: pp. 238-241, 245-249).
3. In slow-switch cases and Frances-style non-switch cases, thinkers cannot know a priori that thoughts involving different external objects or kinds differ in content, and can commit simple invalid inferences through equivocation. Source: pp. 213-217, 221-226 (PDF: pp. 241-245, 249-254).
4. Therefore, any anti-individualist theory, whether Fregean or non-Fregean, must reject the transparency of difference of content and concede that rational agents can fail to grasp simple invalidities a priori. Source: pp. 226-227 (PDF: pp. 254-255) (from 2-3).
5. A theory of rationality that maintains that agents can grasp simple validity and contradiction a priori while denying that they can grasp simple invalidity a priori is philosophically unmotivated and creates an arbitrary epistemic asymmetry. Source: pp. 227-230 (PDF: pp. 255-258).
6. Non-Fregean anti-individualism resolves the asymmetry by symmetrically rejecting both transparency principles and adopting a psychologically realistic conception of blameless error. Source: pp. 230-231 (PDF: pp. 258-259).
7. Therefore, there is a fundamental tension between anti-individualism and Fregean sense: philosophers must either reject Fregean sense to preserve symmetric externalism or reject anti-individualism to preserve Fregean rationality. Source: pp. 230-231 (PDF: pp. 258-259) (from 1, 4-6).


### 7 The Reductio Argument: Transmission of Warrant

#### Section Abstract
Jessica Brown evaluates the consequence problem for compatibilism, centered on Michael McKinsey's reductio argument: if a subject has a priori privileged access to her thoughts and can know a priori via philosophical reflection that externalism entails world-conditions for those thoughts, she could deduce substantive empirical facts about the external environment (such as the existence of water) from the armchair. Brown rejects the claim that armchair knowledge of the world is unproblematic or an anti-skeptical asset. She then undertakes a comprehensive critique of the leading compatibilist defense mounted by Martin Davies and Crispin Wright, who argue that the inference instantiates a failure of the transmission of warrant rather than a failure of epistemic closure. Brown demonstrates that all three candidate justifications for transmission failure collapse: (1) Wright's conditional analysis of warrant invites external-world skepticism, conflicts with reliabilism, and merely relocates armchair entitlement to the conclusion as a prerequisite for self-knowledge; (2) Davies's question-begging analysis fails because a doubter's loss of premise warrant does not demonstrate non-transmission; and (3) Davies's relevant-alternatives account conflates warrant with local reliability, rendering justified false belief impossible and contradicting standard analyses of Gettier cases. Brown concludes that transmission failure cannot rescue compatibilism from the consequence problem.

#### Main Summary

##### 1 Introduction
Brown turns from the *achievement problem* (how privileged access to externally individuated thoughts is possible, resolved in Part I) to the *consequence problem* (what epistemic consequences follow from the combination of privileged access and anti-individualism). The core threat is the *McKinsey reductio* (McKinsey 1991, Boghossian 1997):
- (R1) Sally can know a priori that she thinks that water is wet. *(Privileged access)*
- (R2) Sally can know a priori that if she thinks that water is wet, then her environment contains water. *(Anti-individualism)*
- (R3) Sally can know a priori that her environment contains water. *(Deductive inference from R1 and R2)*
- (R4) But (R3) is absurd: substantive empirical knowledge of natural kinds cannot be acquired from the armchair without empirical investigation.
- (R5) Therefore, anti-individualism and privileged access are incompatible.
Brown rejects attempts to embrace (R3) as an anti-skeptical triumph (Warfield, Sawyer): the fact that Sally already knows empirically that water exists does not make an armchair deductive route to the same conclusion any less absurd, just as having empirical knowledge of natural gas reserves does not make armchair knowledge of natural gas plausible. Brown focuses on the prominent compatibilist response developed by Martin Davies and Crispin Wright, who accept that (R3) is absurd but deny that it follows from (R1) and (R2) by arguing that warrant fails to transmit across the inference.
Source: pp. 233-239 (PDF: pp. 263-269).

##### 2 Closure and Transmission
Brown distinguishes Davies and Wright's position from the radical denial of epistemic closure proposed by Robert Nozick and Fred Dretske. Closure denial holds that an agent can know the premises of a valid deductive argument while failing to know the conclusion (e.g., knowing an animal is a zebra while failing to know it is not a cleverly disguised mule, because belief in the latter is insensitive to truth). Brown demonstrates that closure denial cannot assist the compatibilist: Sally *does* know that water exists, because her interactions with water ground empirical knowledge, and her belief satisfies Nozickean tracking (had water never existed, she would lack water concepts and would not believe water exists). Davies and Wright defend the subtler thesis of *transmission failure*: Sally knows the premises and knows the conclusion, but cannot acquire warrant or knowledge for the conclusion *by inferring it from the premises*. Transmission of warrant requires that warrant for the premises provides the epistemic ground for accepting the conclusion. Davies and Wright identify three potential mechanisms for transmission failure across the water argument, the zebra argument, and Moore's proof of the external world: circularity/conditional warrant, begging the question, and failure to rule out relevant alternatives.
Source: pp. 239-244 (PDF: pp. 269-274).

##### 3 Wright and Conditional Warrant
Brown analyzes Wright's template for transmission failure. Wright argues that an argument of the form $[A, A \to B \vdash B]$ suffers transmission failure when it is implicitly circular, meaning that warrant for premise $A$ is conditional on having prior, independent warrant for conclusion $B$. Wright's template specifies:
1. $A$ entails $B$;
2. There is an incompatible proposition $C$;
3. The subject's warrant for $A$ consists in an experiential state subjectively indistinguishable from one in which $C$ is true;
4. $C$ would be true if $B$ were false.
Under this template, Wright claims that one cannot move from the subjective state to premise $A$ without an antecedent entitlement to discount $C$, which is equivalent to having prior warrant for $B$. Applied to the zebra case, perceptual experience of stripes warrants 'The animal is a zebra' ($A$) only if the subject has independent warrant that it is not a disguised mule ($B$). Applied to McKinsey, a priori warrant for 'I think that water is wet' ($A$) is conditional on prior and independent warrant that 'My environment contains water' ($B$).
Source: pp. 244-247 (PDF: pp. 274-277).

##### 3.1 Challenging the Conditional Analysis
Brown challenges Wright's conditional analysis by offering a minimal, commonsense alternative supported by epistemological externalism. Under ordinary epistemic practice, a perceptual experience as of a zebra directly warrants believing that the animal is a zebra without requiring prior positive evidence against remote skeptical possibilities, provided there is no positive reason to suspect trickery. Brown shows that Wright's demand for independent entitlement reflects an internalist epistemology that externalist anti-individualists reject. On Alvin Goldman's process reliabilism, a perceptual belief is warranted if produced by a reliable belief-forming process, regardless of whether the thinker possesses independent warrants against subjectively indistinguishable skeptical alternatives. While reliabilism accepts that the *truth* of the conclusion is an external condition for the process's reliability, it explicitly denies that having warrant for the premises requires prior *epistemic warrant* for the conclusion.
Source: pp. 247-251 (PDF: pp. 277-281).

##### 3.2 The Conditional Analysis and Skepticism
Brown demonstrates that Wright's conditional analysis generates an acute skeptical hazard. If warrant for ordinary perceptual claims ($O$) requires prior and independent warrant against incompatible skeptical hypotheses ($SK$), subjects will be stripped of ordinary knowledge. Thinkers cannot have empirical evidence against radical hypotheses like the brain-in-a-vat (BIV), since BIV scenarios are defined to be empirically indistinguishable from veridical reality. Nor can one plausibly claim a non-evidential "standing entitlement" against mundane skeptical hypotheses (such as painted mules) where empirical evidence is readily obtainable yet ordinarily ungathered. By making ordinary warrant hostage to antecedent skeptical refutation, Wright's conditional analysis collapses into external-world skepticism.
Source: pp. 251-254 (PDF: pp. 281-284).

##### 3.3 A Priori Entitlement
Brown delivers a fatal dilemma against Wright's resolution of the McKinsey reductio. If Sally's a priori warrant for premise (W1) ('I think that water is wet') requires prior and independent warrant for conclusion (W3) ('My environment contains water'), what is the epistemic status of that prior warrant?
- If the prior warrant is empirical, Sally's warrant for (W1) becomes empirical, destroying privileged access.
- If the prior warrant is a priori—as Wright explicitly maintains, positing an "a priori entitlement to discount illusions of content"—then compatibilism remains committed to the claim that subjects have an armchair, non-empirical warrant for substantive environmental facts.
Brown points out that what makes the McKinsey reductio absurd is that thinkers possess armchair warrant for substantive world facts, not merely that they derive it via a specific three-step syllogism. Positing an unargued, non-inferential a priori entitlement to the existence of water is every bit as absurd as inferring it from self-knowledge, leaving the core reductio unresolved.
Source: pp. 254-258 (PDF: pp. 284-288).

##### 4 Begging the Question
Brown evaluates Davies's contention that warrant fails to transmit because the reductio argument begs the question in Frank Jackson's sense: anyone who doubts the conclusion would hold background beliefs relative to which the evidence for the premises ceases to be evidence. In the BIV case, a thinker who doubts she is unenvatted will not treat perceptual appearances as warrant for seeing a table. Brown refutes this defense (following Helen Beebee): if doubting the conclusion undermines the subject's warrant for the premise, then the subject simply lacks warrant for the premise in that context. This does not show that when an ordinary thinker *does* possess warrant for the premises, that warrant fails to transmit to the conclusion. A failure of premise warrant under skeptical doubt is entirely compatible with full transmission of warrant in non-skeptical contexts.
Source: pp. 258-259 (PDF: pp. 288-289).

##### 5 Warrant and Ruling Out Alternatives
Brown examines Davies's primary positive account: warrant fails to transmit when the evidence supporting the premises fails to rule out the salient or relevant alternatives to the conclusion. In Davies's "goal argument," perceptual evidence of players and crowds warrants 'A goal has been scored' ($G1$) and conceptual analysis warrants the conditional ($G2$), but fails to warrant 'This is not a movie scene' ($G3$) because it cannot rule out that salient alternative. Davies formalizes this as an epistemic requirement: evidence warrants a proposition $p$ only if it rules out the relevant alternatives to $p$. Brown refutes this theory by demonstrating its unacceptable epistemological costs:
1. *Destruction of justified false belief*: In Dancy's Gettier case, Julie watches a BBC tennis broadcast and believes (McE1) 'I have just seen McEnroe win the Wimbledon final', inferring (McE3) 'McEnroe is this year's Wimbledon champion'. Because the BBC broadcast was an unannounced recorded repeat of last year's final, (McE1) is false, though (McE3) happens to be true. On Davies's model, because Julie's visual evidence cannot rule out the relevant alternative that she is watching a repeat, she lacks warrant for both (McE1) and (McE3). This contradicts the near-universal consensus in epistemology that Julie's beliefs are justified though unknowledgeable.
2. *Impossibility of false warrant*: More generally, whenever a belief is false, the actual state of affairs ($\neg p$) is an uneliminated relevant alternative compatible with the subject's evidence; under Davies's rule, no false belief could ever be justified or warranted.
3. *Conflation of warrant with knowledge*: Brown explains that Davies has illicitly imported a condition on *knowledge* (Goldman's local reliability, which explains why Gettier cases fail to be knowledge) into the definition of *warrant/justification*. Both internalist accounts (mental accessibility) and externalist reliabilist accounts (Goldman's conditional reliability of deductive inference) preserve justified false beliefs and reject Davies's restriction.
Source: pp. 259-269 (PDF: pp. 289-299).

##### 6 Conclusion
Brown concludes that the transmission-failure strategy developed by Davies and Wright fails to defend compatibilism against the consequence problem. Limiting transmission cannot be sustained on grounds of implicit circularity, question-begging, or relevant-alternative elimination without collapsing into skepticism or dismantling justified belief. If compatibilism is to be rescued from the McKinsey reductio, it must be by attacking the conditional premise (R2)—demonstrating in Chapter 8 that anti-individualism does not license a priori knowable entailments from thoughts to specific external environments.
Source: pp. 269-270 (PDF: pp. 299-300).

#### Important Concepts

- **The consequence problem.**
  Definition: Martin Davies's classification of the epistemic challenge examining what substantive metaphysical and environmental consequences follow from the conjunction of anti-individualism and privileged access, distinct from the achievement problem of how self-knowledge is acquired.
  Source: pp. 234-235 (PDF: pp. 264-265).

- **The McKinsey reductio.**
  Definition: Michael McKinsey's incompatibilist argument demonstrating that combining a priori self-knowledge of thought contents with an a priori externalist entailment from thoughts to environmental conditions yields the absurd consequence that thinkers can achieve armchair a priori knowledge of empirical worldly facts.
  Source: pp. 235-237 (PDF: pp. 265-267).

- **Transmission of warrant vs. epistemic closure.**
  Definition: Epistemic closure asserts that knowing the premises and validity of an argument entails knowing the conclusion; transmission of warrant asserts that warrant for the premises transfers across the deductive inference to provide the epistemic ground for accepting the conclusion.
  Source: pp. 239-244 (PDF: pp. 269-274).

- **Wright's conditional analysis.**
  Definition: Crispin Wright's template for transmission failure, maintaining that an argument is implicitly circular and non-transmissive whenever warrant for the premise is conditional on possessing antecedent, independent warrant for the conclusion.
  Source: pp. 244-247 (PDF: pp. 274-277).

- **Relevant alternatives condition on warrant.**
  Definition: Martin Davies's epistemic requirement holding that a body of evidence constitutes warrant for a proposition only if it rules out all salient or relevant counter-hypotheses, a condition Brown demonstrates conflates warrant with local reliability and eliminates justified false belief.
  Source: pp. 259-265, 267-269 (PDF: pp. 289-295, 297-299).

#### Formal-Logical Argument Reconstruction: The Failure of the Transmission-Failure Defense against the McKinsey Reductio
1. The McKinsey reductio threatens compatibilism by deducing absurd armchair environmental knowledge from privileged access and anti-individualist entailments via valid deductive inference. Source: pp. 235-237 (PDF: pp. 265-267).
2. The transmission-failure strategy attempts to block the reductio by arguing that warrant fails to transmit across the inference due to circularity (Wright), question-begging (Davies), or failure to rule out alternatives (Davies). Source: pp. 243-244, 258-261 (PDF: pp. 273-274, 288-291).
3. Wright's circularity model (conditional analysis) either requires prior a priori warrant for the environmental conclusion—reinstating armchair knowledge—or invites global perceptual skepticism by demanding evidence against uneliminated skeptical scenarios. Source: pp. 251-258 (PDF: pp. 281-288).
4. Davies's question-begging model establishes only that skeptical doubters lack premise warrant, which fails to show that non-doubters with warranted premises suffer non-transmission. Source: pp. 258-259 (PDF: pp. 288-289).
5. Davies's relevant-alternatives model conflates warrant with knowledge, rendering justified false belief impossible and contradicting standard analyses of Gettier cases. Source: pp. 261-269 (PDF: pp. 291-299).
6. Therefore, none of the proposed mechanisms establish a failure of transmission of warrant for the McKinsey inference. Source: pp. 269-270 (PDF: pp. 299-300) (from 2-5).
7. Therefore, compatibilism cannot be defended against the consequence problem by limiting the transmission of warrant; any viable defense must demonstrate that anti-individualism does not entail a priori conditionals from thoughts to specific environmental conditions. Source: pp. 269-270 (PDF: pp. 299-300) (from 1, 6).


### 8 The Reductio Argument: Entailments between Thought and the World

#### Section Abstract
Jessica Brown dismantles the consequence problem by mounting a decisive challenge against the second premise of Michael McKinsey's reductio argument—the claim that anti-individualism yields a priori knowable entailments from a subject's thoughts to specific external environmental conditions. Brown demonstrates that standard Twin Earth thought experiments establish only the counterfactual dependence of content on the environment, not a priori necessary conditionals. While anti-individualism can be formulated as conditional principles linking psychological states to external facts, a subject can never know a priori that she satisfies the antecedents of these conditionals. In natural kind and singular anti-individualism, bridging conditionals require the subject to know a priori that she is not in a "bad case" (suffering an illusion of thought or entertaining a descriptive thought), which is impossible because beliefs about avoiding the bad case fail Nozickean sensitivity. In social anti-individualism, bridging conditionals require a priori knowledge that the thinker is "agnostic" about a concept's boundaries, which is unavailable given the epistemic opacity of conceptual determinacy and the ubiquity of incomplete understanding. In the Appendix, Brown contrasts her sensitivity-based denial of knowledge transmission across cogito-to-thought-existence inferences with Davies and Wright's warrant-based frameworks, demonstrating that Davies is covertly committed to denying epistemic closure while Wright's view reinstates absurd armchair knowledge.

#### Main Summary

##### 1 Introduction
Brown pursues the definitive compatibilist resolution of the consequence problem: refuting premise (R2) of the McKinsey reductio, which asserts that Sally can know a priori via anti-individualism that if she thinks that water is wet, her environment contains water. Drawing on a significant development in her own thinking (reversing Brown 1995 and 2001), Brown argues that knowledge of anti-individualist philosophical arguments does not license a priori conditionals linking specific thoughts to external environments ($p \to E$). First, Twin Earth scenarios establish only counterfactual dependence (had the environment differed, thoughts would have differed), not necessary entailment: Tyler Burge explicitly acknowledges that a thinker could entertain the concept *water* in a dry world if she possessed chemical theory ($H_2O$) or acquired the term from a linguistic community. Second, while externalism supports conditional principles linking psychological conditions to the world, thinkers cannot know a priori that they meet the antecedents of those conditionals.
Source: pp. 271-274 (PDF: pp. 303-306).

##### 2 Natural Kind Anti-Individualism
Brown evaluates natural kind anti-individualism in isolation from social externalism. To bridge thought and world, an externalist might propose:
- Principle (A): *If subject $S$ has a concept naming natural kind $k_i$ and is chemically indifferent (ignorant of its underlying microstructure), then $S$'s environment contains instances of $k_i$.*
Brown shows Sally cannot use (A) in the reductio because she cannot know a priori that 'water' actually names a natural kind: on Motley Earth (where watery liquid is a composite of diverse kinds) or Dry Earth (where liquid is an environmental illusion), Sally would still believe 'water' names a kind, proving her belief fails Nozickean tracking. Historical examples ('phlogiston', 'jade') confirm that kind-success is an empirical discovery.
To salvage the reductio, Paul Boghossian reformulates the antecedent in terms of subjective intention:
- Principle ($B'$): *If chemically indifferent $S$ intends $t$ to name a natural kind and does not suffer an illusion of thought, then $t$ names a natural kind.* (Illusion view)
- Principle ($C'$): *If chemically indifferent $S$ intends $t$ to name a natural kind and $t$ does not express a descriptive concept, then $t$ names a natural kind.* (Descriptive view)
While Sally knows a priori that she intends 'water' to name a natural kind and is chemically indifferent, Brown demonstrates that Sally cannot know a priori that she is not in a *bad case* (that she is neither suffering an illusion of thought nor thinking descriptively). Were Sally on Dry or Motley Earth, she would still falsely believe she is thinking a non-illusory, non-descriptive natural kind thought. Because her belief that she avoids the bad case is insensitive, it does not constitute a priori knowledge.
Source: pp. 274-284 (PDF: pp. 306-316).

##### 2.1 Privileged Access and the Bad Case
Brown refutes Boghossian's contention that privileged access guarantees a priori knowledge of whether a term expresses a concept. Privileged access guarantees only that if an agent thinks that $p$, she can know a priori that she thinks that $p$. This standard Cartesian warrant is self-verifying: if an agent self-ascribes a thought, the second-order judgment carries the content of the first-order thought. However, the belief 'I am not in a bad case' (or 'I am not suffering an illusion of thought') is *not* self-verifying: an agent suffering a total illusion of thought would still judge 'I am not suffering an illusion of thought'.
Brown considers whether a subject could infer she avoids the bad case via a valid deduction:
- (T1) I think that water is wet. *(Privileged access)*
- (T2) If I think that water is wet, then I am having a thought rather than an illusion of thought. *(Conceptual analysis)*
- (T3) Therefore, I am having a thought rather than an illusion of thought.
Brown establishes that knowledge fails to transmit across this inference: while Sally's belief in (T1) satisfies sensitivity (if she had no water thoughts, she would not self-ascribe them), her belief in (T3) violates sensitivity (if she were suffering an illusion of thought, she would still believe T3). Thus, (T1)–(T3) constitutes a genuine counterexample to the transmission and closure of knowledge, precluding armchair knowledge that one avoids the bad case.
Source: pp. 284-290 (PDF: pp. 316-322).

##### 3 Singular Anti-Individualism
Brown applies the conditional critique to singular anti-individualism (perceptual demonstrative thoughts). The proposed reductio syllogism:
- (P1) Sally knows a priori that she thinks that that apple is red.
- (P2) Sally knows a priori that if she thinks that that apple is red, then that apple exists.
- (P3) Therefore, Sally knows a priori that that apple exists.
Brown demonstrates that (P2) cannot be known a priori under either formulation of the antecedent:
1. *Strong formulation (actual perception)*: If the condition requires actually seeing and referring to an object, Sally cannot know a priori that she is perceiving rather than hallucinating.
2. *Weak formulation (apparent perception)*: If formulated as seeming to see and attempting to refer, anti-individualism yields:
   - ($D'$): *If $S$ seems to see an object, attempts to refer demonstratively, and does not suffer an illusion of thought, then an object exists.* (Illusion view)
   - ($E'$): *If $S$ seems to see an object, attempts to refer demonstratively, and does not have a descriptive thought, then an object exists.* (Descriptive view)
Just as in natural kind cases, Sally cannot know a priori that she avoids the bad case (illusion or descriptive fallback), defeating the singular reductio.
Source: pp. 290-294 (PDF: pp. 322-326).

##### 4 Social Anti-Individualism
Brown examines whether social anti-individualism licenses an armchair deduction of the existence of a linguistic community. Burge's arthritis thought experiment stipulates a linguistic community and shows only counterfactual variation. However, Brown formulates a conditional principle derived from social externalism:
- Principle (F): *If subject $S$ has concept $c$ and is agnostic about $c$, then $S$ is part of a linguistic community with concept $c$.*
Brown distinguishes *agnosticism* (uncertainty about whether a concept applies to borderline cases where there is a determinate fact of the matter) from *misunderstanding* (false beliefs about application conditions). Brown proves (F) holds: an isolated, solitary individual who applies 'sofa' to couches but is unsure about large armchairs cannot have the concept *sofa*, because absent other speakers or natural kind essences, there is no metaphysical fact fixing the concept's boundary beyond the subject's own dispositions; hence an isolated subject cannot be agnostic.
However, Brown reveals that Sally cannot use (F) in a reductio because she cannot know a priori that she meets the antecedent—she cannot know a priori that she is *agnostic*. Sally can know introspectively that she is unsure whether 'sofa' applies to armchairs, but she cannot know a priori whether there is a determinate social fact settling the boundary or whether the term is vague/indeterminate. Furthermore, social anti-individualism establishes the ubiquity of incomplete understanding, making it an uneliminated relevant alternative that Sally misunderstands the concept rather than being cleanly agnostic.
Source: pp. 294-300 (PDF: pp. 326-332).

##### 5 Combined Views
Brown evaluates whether combining natural kind and social anti-individualism into a unified thesis resurrects the reductio (revisiting Brown 1995):
- Principle (G): *If subject $S$ has concept $c$ and is agnostic about $c$, then either $c$ is a natural kind concept and $S$'s environment contains instances of that kind, or $S$ is part of a linguistic community with concept $c$.*
Brown demonstrates that (G) fails for the identical reason: exploiting (G) requires a priori knowledge of agnosticism, which social externalism systematically renders opaque. Thus, combining externalist theories provides no loophole for the consequence argument.
Source: pp. 300-302 (PDF: pp. 332-334).

##### 6 Conclusion
Brown synthesizes the findings of Chapter 8. The McKinsey reductio fails because anti-individualism supports no a priori knowable entailments from thoughts to external environments. Twin Earth thought experiments establish only counterfactual dependence. Bridging conditionals require antecedents that subjects cannot know a priori without empirical investigation into the external world. Compatibilism is completely vindicated against the consequence problem.
Source: pp. 302-303 (PDF: pp. 334-335).

##### Appendix to Chapter 8: Comparison with Davies and Wright
Brown clarifies her philosophical commitments relative to Davies and Wright:
1. *Transmission failure*: Brown affirms that knowledge fails to transmit across (T1)–(T3), but grounds this strictly on Nozickean sensitivity (a condition on *knowledge*), avoiding Davies and Wright's problematic restrictions on the transmission of *warrant*.
2. *Water argument contrasted*: Brown's sensitivity account does *not* imply transmission failure across the water argument (W1)–(W3), because (W3) ('My environment contains water') is sensitive: in a waterless world, Sally would lack water concepts and would not believe W3.
3. *Epistemic closure*: Davies's "architecturalism argument" (attempting to block a priori knowledge of the Language of Thought from self-knowledge and cognitive science) reveals that Davies cannot avoid denying epistemic closure of warrant and knowledge. Conversely, Wright's conditional analysis avoids denying closure only by positing an antecedent a priori entitlement to the environmental conclusion, which directly concedes the absurd consequence that thinkers possess armchair knowledge of the external world.
Source: pp. 303-308 (PDF: pp. 335-340).

#### Important Concepts

- **The "bad case."**
  Definition: Brown's unified classification of counterfactual externalist scenarios in which an apparent natural kind or singular demonstrative thought either fails to express any thought content at all (the illusion view) or expresses an unintended descriptive thought (the descriptive view).
  Source: pp. 282-284, 292-293 (PDF: pp. 314-316, 324-325).

- **Chemically indifferent subject.**
  Definition: A thinker who possesses and deploys an atomic natural kind concept (such as water or gold) while remaining completely ignorant of its fundamental microstructural or chemical constitution.
  Source: pp. 275-276 (PDF: pp. 307-308).

- **Agnosticism vs. misunderstanding.**
  Definition: Tyler Burge's distinction between incomplete understanding where a thinker holds false application beliefs (misunderstanding, e.g., arthritis in the thigh) versus where a thinker is genuinely unsure about application to a category where there exists an objective, determinate fact of the matter (agnosticism).
  Source: pp. 295-298 (PDF: pp. 327-330).

- **Self-verifying second-order judgments.**
  Definition: The Cartesian mechanism whereby cogito-like judgments ('I think that $p$') are automatically true and sensitive, which Brown demonstrates cannot be extended to judgments about avoiding the bad case or possessing non-illusory thoughts.
  Source: pp. 286-288 (PDF: pp. 318-320).

- **Davies's architecturalism argument.**
  Definition: Martin Davies's reductio argument examining whether combining privileged access with a priori philosophical defenses of the Language of Thought (LOT) hypothesis yields illicit armchair knowledge of the internal physical architecture of the brain.
  Source: pp. 305-307 (PDF: pp. 337-339).

#### Formal-Logical Argument Reconstruction: The Failure of A Priori Entailments between Thought and World
1. The McKinsey reductio succeeds only if an agent can know a priori via anti-individualism that thinking a specific thought entails specific external environmental conditions (Premise R2). Source: pp. 271-274 (PDF: pp. 303-306).
2. Standard Twin Earth arguments establish only counterfactual dependence (if the environment were different, thoughts would differ), not necessary conditionals, because concepts can be sustained by linguistic communities or microstructural knowledge in the absence of the kind. Source: pp. 273-274 (PDF: pp. 305-306).
3. Bridging conditionals for natural kind and singular thoughts require that the subject is not in a bad case (neither suffering an illusion of thought nor thinking descriptively). Source: pp. 279-282, 292-294 (PDF: pp. 311-314, 324-326).
4. A subject cannot know a priori that she is not in a bad case, because in Dry Earth or hallucinatory scenarios she would still believe she is not in a bad case, violating Nozickean sensitivity. Source: pp. 282-284, 286-290 (PDF: pp. 314-316, 318-322).
5. Bridging conditionals for social anti-individualism require that the subject knows a priori that she is agnostic about the concept's application conditions. Source: pp. 294-298 (PDF: pp. 326-330).
6. A subject cannot know a priori that she is agnostic, because whether a concept's boundaries are determinate and whether she avoids misunderstanding are empirically uneliminated alternatives. Source: pp. 298-300 (PDF: pp. 330-332).
7. Therefore, anti-individualism does not support a priori knowable entailments from thoughts to external environments, Premise R2 of the McKinsey reductio is false, and compatibilism is fully vindicated. Source: pp. 302-303 (PDF: pp. 334-335) (from 1-6).


### 9 Conclusion

#### Section Abstract
Jessica Brown synthesizes the overarching conclusions of the monograph. Anti-individualism lacks the radical epistemological consequences commonly attributed to it: it does not undermine privileged access to thought contents (the achievement problem is solved by demonstrating that alternative counterfactuals are irrelevant), and it does not license an absurd a priori route to substantive knowledge of the external world (the consequence problem is solved because externalism yields no a priori knowable entailments from thought to world). While anti-individualism does undermine the transparency of content, this refutes only an over-idealized conception of rationality ("B-rationality"), not rational agency itself. Crucially, the failure of transparency generates an insurmountable philosophical tension with modern Fregean object-dependent senses, establishing that philosophers of mind must choose between anti-individualism and Fregean psychological explanation.

#### Main Summary

##### Conclusion
Brown reviews the three core epistemic problems examined across the monograph:
1. *The Achievement Problem*: Anti-individualism is compatible with privileged access. Counterfactual twin and no-reference scenarios are not relevant alternatives, resolving the tension under both discriminative and reliabilist theories of knowledge.
2. *Anti-Individualism and Reasoning*: Anti-individualism undermines content transparency (sameness for non-Fregeans; difference for all externalists). This refutes Boghossian's idealized "B-rationality" (a priori logical omniscience) while preserving ordinary rationality, blameless logical error, and folk-psychological explanation.
3. *The Consequence Problem*: The McKinsey reductio fails because anti-individualism supports no a priori knowable entailments from thoughts to external environments.
Finally, Brown shows anti-individualism conflicts with Fregean object-dependent sense, forcing a choice between externalism and Fregean psychological explanation.
Source: pp. 309-312 (PDF: pp. 343-345).

#### Important Concepts

- **Epistemological benignity of anti-individualism.**
  Definition: Jessica Brown's master thesis that anti-individualism has neither skeptical consequences for self-knowledge nor absurd anti-skeptical consequences for armchair environmental knowledge.
  Source: pp. 309-312 (PDF: pp. 343-345).

- **Division between externalism and Fregean psychological explanation.**
  Definition: The conclusion that externalist content cannot be reconciled with Fregean senses, requiring theorists to develop non-Fregean models of action explanation, informativeness, and attitude opacity.
  Source: pp. 311-312 (PDF: pp. 345-346).

#### Formal-Logical Argument Reconstruction: The Epistemological Benignity of Anti-Individualism
1. If anti-individualism has radical epistemological consequences, it must either undermine privileged access to thought contents (the achievement problem), undermine rational agency (the reasoning problem), or yield absurd armchair knowledge of the external world (the consequence problem). Source: pp. 309-311 (PDF: pp. 343-345).
2. The achievement problem is defused because counterfactual switch scenarios are not relevant alternatives, preserving privileged access under both reliabilist and discriminative epistemic standards. Source: pp. 309-310 (PDF: pp. 343-344).
3. The reasoning problem is defused because the failure of transparency refutes only an untenable, idealized model of rationality (B-rationality) while preserving folk-psychological explanation and blameless logical agency. Source: pp. 310-311 (PDF: pp. 344-345).
4. The consequence problem is defused because anti-individualism yields only counterfactual dependencies rather than a priori knowable entailments from thoughts to external environments. Source: pp. 310-311 (PDF: pp. 344-345).
5. Therefore, anti-individualism lacks radical epistemological consequences, vindicating externalist compatibilism across mind and world. Source: pp. 311-312 (PDF: pp. 345-346) (from 1-4).
