# Boghossian 2011 - The transparency of mental content revisited

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Boghossian-2011-The Transparency of Mental Content Revisited-Philosophical Studies.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Boghossian-2011-The Transparency of Mental Content Revisited-Philosophical Studies|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

Boghossian revisits his transparency-based challenge to anti-individualist theories of mental content in response to Robert Stalnaker’s thoroughly externalist and contextual alternative. After restating how failures of transparency threaten to collapse the difference between factual ignorance and logical irrationality, he considers Stalnaker’s proposal to preserve rationality by attributing tacit, validity-restoring presuppositions. Boghossian argues that this strategy cannot be transferred satisfactorily to slow-switching cases because its account of rational acceptance is circular. He closes by arguing that Stalnaker’s contextualism appears to presuppose attributor-independent contents somewhere in the attributional chain and thus cannot dispense entirely with Intentional Realism. Source: pp. 457–465.

## Main Summary

### 1 Our residual Cartesianism

Stalnaker diagnoses many philosophy-of-mind puzzles as residues of Cartesianism and proposes a more thoroughly externalist conception of mentality. Boghossian focuses on the puzzle generated by transparency. Transparency of sameness requires a thinker to be able to know a priori when two token thoughts have the same content; transparency of difference imposes the parallel requirement for distinct contents. Here “a priori” excludes dependence on outer experience but permits introspection. Boghossian’s earlier contention was that violations of either principle can make an intuitively rational thinker who merely lacks empirical information appear to commit a simple logical fallacy. Because Burge- and Putnam-style anti-individualism generates such violations, it threatens the distinction between errors of fact and errors of reasoning that any adequate account of content should preserve. Stalnaker accepts that desideratum but argues that anti-individualism can satisfy a suitably qualified transparency principle once both knowledge and content attribution are understood externalistically rather than as access to inner representational vehicles. Source: pp. 457–458.

### 2 Stalnaker on slow switching

The slow-switching case concerns Peter, who is unknowingly transported from Earth to Twin Earth. After enough time, some of his “water” thoughts acquire twater content, while memory-based thoughts about Earth plausibly retain water content. Peter may therefore combine a water premise with a twater premise in the apparently valid-looking (Water) inference and reach a conclusion that equivocates between the two contents. Boghossian’s point is that Peter seems empirically uninformed, not logically irrational. Tye avoids mixed contents by claiming that twater displaces water; Burge instead appeals to preservative memory to ensure that all tokens in the inference carry one content. Boghossian rejects the first as an implausible theory of concept attribution and the second because content assignment would vary with the order in which Peter thinks the premises. Source: pp. 458–460.

Stalnaker locates the relevant shift not simply within Peter but partly in the context of attribution. On his view, content ascriptions explain agents’ capacities and dispositions by locating the world as they take it to be within a space of relevant alternatives. Attributors may use different world-involving descriptive resources for different explanatory purposes. Transparency is then secured when an apt description of a thinker’s rational capacities represents that thinker’s world in a transparency-preserving way, not through introspective acquaintance with an essentially content-bearing inner object. Source: pp. 460–461.

### 3 Discussion of Stalnaker’s alternative approach to the mental content

Boghossian denies that his target view requires word-like inner vehicles identifiable independently of their meanings. Its internal component need claim only that thinkers can introspectively register their occurrent thoughts. Nor does concept talk entail a language-of-thought architecture: concepts may instead be propositional constituents. He formulates the “Common View” as the conjunction of Intentional Realism—the existence of attributor-independent facts about what contents a thinker has—and Fregeanism—the view that contents involve modes of presentation rather than actual objects and properties. Twin Earth arguments, he contends, presuppose both: their surprising conclusion is not merely that reference is externally individuated, but that even grasp of Fregean sense is externally individuated. Stalnaker’s strategy is therefore best understood as rejecting the Common View, rather than anti-individualism, as the source of the transparency problem. Source: pp. 461–463.

### (Ship)

Stalnaker’s most adaptable case involves John, who points to the bow of one ship and the stern of another, judges that “this ship” is an aircraft carrier and that “this ship” is British, and concludes that a British aircraft carrier is present. Stalnaker proposes representing John as tacitly presupposing that the two ships are identical. That false suppressed premise restores validity without requiring John to have considered their distinctness. Applied to Peter, the analogous move attributes the presupposition that twater is water. Source: pp. 463–464.

Boghossian objects that presupposition attribution must be constrained. Peter currently lacks the conceptual and linguistic resources needed to understand that twater is not water; he could accept this only after instruction. Yet the mere fact that someone would rationally accept a proposition after instruction does not show that the person already presupposes it, as an incomplete mathematical proof illustrates. More seriously, the account becomes circular: Peter’s rational acceptance is assessed by whether he avoids simple logical errors, while the attributed presupposition is introduced precisely to establish that his apparent equivocation is not such an error. The strategy therefore fails to explain how anti-individualism can respect transparency. Source: pp. 464–465.

Finally, Boghossian challenges Stalnaker’s general contextualism. If A’s content depends on attributor B’s purposes and beliefs, then the account already assumes determinate facts about B’s intentional contents. Treating those facts as dependent on further attributors threatens regress or instability; fixing any of them independently concedes Intentional Realism for at least some contents. Stalnaker must consequently exempt some contents from contextual treatment or show that applying contextualism across the board avoids this instability. Source: p. 465.

### Contextual Content Attribution Presupposes Intentional Realism *(p. 465)*

1. If every intentional state's content depends on an attributor's variable purposes and beliefs, then content attribution generates a vicious regress.
2. It is not the case that a stable account of content attribution generates a vicious regress.
3. If both (if every intentional state's content depends on an attributor's variable purposes and beliefs, then content attribution generates a vicious regress) and (it is not the case that a stable account of content attribution generates a vicious regress), then on every stable account of content attribution, some intentional states have determinate, interpreter-independent representational contents.
4. Both (if every intentional state's content depends on an attributor's variable purposes and beliefs, then content attribution generates a vicious regress) and (it is not the case that a stable account of content attribution generates a vicious regress).
5. Therefore, on every stable account of content attribution, some intentional states have determinate, interpreter-independent representational contents.
