# Hauser-Naeem-2024-Phenomenal Transparency and the Boundary of Cognition

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Hauser-Naeem-2024-Phenomenal Transparency and the Boundary of Cognition-Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Hauser-Naeem-2024-Phenomenal Transparency and the Boundary of Cognition|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

Phenomenal transparency was once widely believed to be necessary for cognitive extension. Recently, this claim has come under attack, with a new consensus coalescing around the idea that transparency is neither necessary for internal nor extended cognitive processes. We take these recent critiques as an opportunity to refine the concept of transparency relevant for cognitive extension. In particular, we highlight that transparency concerns an agent’s employment of a resource – and that employment is compatible with an agent consciously apprehending (or attending to) a resource. This means it is possible for an object to be transparent and opaque to an agent, even at a single moment time. Once we understand transparency in this way, the detractors’ claims lose their bite, and existing arguments for transparency’s necessity for cognitive extension return to apply with full force.
Source: PDF p. 1.

## Main Summary

### 1 Introduction

Early extended-cognition theorists treated phenomenal transparency as necessary for extending cognition into an external resource: in fluid skilled use, attention is directed through the resource toward the task, and the resource falls on the subject side of the subject–world boundary. Hauser and Naeem distinguish this issue from choosing among parity-based, complementarity-based, and predictive-processing accounts, while committing to the now-standard priority of extended processes over extended objects. Recent critics argue that transparency cannot be necessary because internal cognition, paradigmatic external cognition, and skilled action can involve conscious attention to their resources. The authors identify a shared premise behind these critiques: consciously apprehending a resource is assumed to rule out transparently employing it. They reject this inference. Transparency concerns whether employing the resource for a cognitive end relies on consciously apprehending its vehicle properties, not whether the resource can simultaneously appear as an object of consciousness. Source: PDF pp. 1–4.

### 2 Transparency and cognitive extension

Heidegger’s carpenter and Merleau-Ponty’s cane user illustrate transparent employment: the skilled agent focuses on the table or the distal environment rather than the hammer or tactile stimulation. Transparency is relational and practical—not an intrinsic property of equipment—because the issue is how an agent employs a resource. Clark, Wheeler, and Thompson and Stapleton connect such employment to cognitive incorporation: what is used to perceive or act belongs to the body-as-subject, while what is perceived or acted upon lies on the world side of the boundary. Tactile-visual sensory substitution clarifies the transition. A novice consciously interprets tactile stimuli and device mechanics, whereas an expert directly experiences environmental objects through the device. On Grush and Springle’s model, learning produces internal models that map neural signals to increasingly distal causes, bypassing intermediary links. Those bypassed links become phenomenally transparent and help locate the experienced subject–world interface farther along the causal chain. Source: PDF pp. 4–6.

### 3 Arguments against transparency in extension

The authors group objections into three families. Parity arguments claim that because internal cognitive processes can be consciously apprehended—as with neurofeedback or careful Inga’s attention to her memory—transparency cannot be required externally. Paradigm-case arguments cite a brain in a vat or Tetris: the external resource remains cognitively operative while being consciously perceived. Complementarity arguments hold that incorporated skills can include focal attention, as expert athletes consciously monitor performance cues. Although these arguments differ, each treats conscious apprehension as sufficient for opacity. Yet the cases establish only that agents attend to a resource, not that the cognitive employment of that resource depends on conscious manipulation of its vehicle properties. Consequently, if transparent employment and conscious apprehension can coexist, none of the cases yet shows an extended process that lacks transparency. Source: PDF pp. 6–10.

### 4 Transparency and cognitive extension, revisited

Hauser and Naeem refine transparency by distinguishing two ways of realizing content with a resource. An expert microscopist can pursue the specimen directly while automatic sensorimotor routines handle the instrument; a novice must consciously manage knobs, focus, and other vehicle properties as intermediate tasks. Likewise, an expert sensory-substitution user directly perceives spatial layout, whereas a novice interprets tactile stimulation and device function. Transparent employment therefore consists in realizing the intended cognitive content without consciously manipulating the resource’s physical or vehicle properties.

This account permits simultaneous transparency and opacity. A cognitive scientist can use her own TVSS device effortlessly to perceive a monitor while the monitor displays states of that very device. She apprehends the device as an object of study, but that apprehension does not enter into or alter the automatic process by which she sees through it. Variants in which the displayed device belongs to someone else, or is mistakenly believed to, reinforce the phenomenological point: awareness of the resource does not by itself determine how it is employed. A resource is transparently employed when its vehicle properties need not be consciously manipulated to produce the relevant content, even if the same resource is also consciously observed. Source: PDF pp. 10–12.

### 5 Replying to others

The consequences of transparency depend on the theory of extension. On dispositional accounts, an agent need only be disposed to use a resource transparently; occasional opaque use need not eliminate an extended dispositional state. On dynamical accounts, extension expands and contracts with episodes of transparent use. Either framework can therefore treat transparency as necessary without claiming that every momentary shift in attention redraws the cognitive boundary. Source: PDF pp. 12–13.

The refined account answers parity cases because Inga’s self-monitoring and passive neurofeedback do not change the transparent processes by which memory and ordinary cognition operate. Active neurofeedback control of alpha waves is genuinely opaque insofar as the agent uses consciously apprehended vehicle properties. This does not expel the brain wholesale from cognition, however, because the same resource simultaneously sustains many other transparent processes. The proper unit is the process, not the object. The brain-in-a-vat case receives the same treatment. Source: PDF pp. 13–14.

Tetris similarly comprises distinct processes. Consciously judging where a zoid fits is opaque, but pressing a control to rotate it for epistemic advantage can be automatic and transparent; one computer thus supports both kinds of process. Expert cricket also combines conscious attention to opponent and ball with transparent control of bat, stance, and movement. These examples show that a resource may participate in transparent and opaque processes at once. To refute transparency’s necessity, critics would need a clear case of cognitive extension in which all relevant processes depend on conscious manipulation of vehicle properties. Their examples instead demonstrate only partial opacity. Source: PDF pp. 14–16.

### 6 Closing remarks

The proposal presupposes a process-based account: whether an object belongs to cognitive machinery is derivative from the cognitive status of processes running through it. At the resource level, transparency can consequently be graded according to the balance of transparent and opaque processes, and cognitive incorporation can also admit degrees. At the level of each process, however, the phenomenological condition remains strict: an extended process cannot rely on conscious manipulation of the resource’s vehicle properties. Existing subject–world-boundary arguments for transparency remain compatible with this refinement because different concurrent processes can place that boundary differently. The authors do not offer a new positive proof that transparency is necessary; they argue that recent counterexamples fail, leaving existing necessity arguments unimpeded. Conscious apprehension of a resource and opaque employment in some processes do not preclude transparent employment—and possible cognitive extension—in others. Source: PDF pp. 16–18.

## Concepts

- **Phenomenal Transparency** (single-source): `Phenomenal Transparency` is a vehicle-level property in the philosophy of extended cognition concerning how an agent experiences and employs an external tool or artifact while performing a cognitive process. (pp. 4, 10–12, 17–18)
  - **Disappearance-from-Apprehension Phenomenal Transparency Definition** (single-source): Phenomenal transparency denotes the total disappearance of an external artifact or cognitive resource from conscious apprehension during use. (pp. 7–10)
  - **Process-Relative Non-Manipulation Phenomenal Transparency Definition** (single-source): Both phenomenal transparency denotes process-relative employment of a cognitive resource without conscious manipulation of its vehicle properties and if (possibly, some external artifact employed in perceptual cognitive extension does not disappear from conscious apprehension), then conscious-apprehension objections fail against process-relative phenomenal transparency. (pp. 10–12, 14–18)
- **Transparent vs. Opaque Employment** (single-source): Transparent employment realizes intended cognitive content through a resource without consciously manipulating its physical or vehicle properties, whereas opaque employment treats manipulation of vehicle properties as an intermediate task. (pp. 10–12)
- **Dispositional vs. Dynamical Cognitive Extension** (single-source): Dispositional extension locates cognitive extension in a standing disposition to employ a resource transparently, whereas dynamical extension treats the cognitive boundary as expanding and contracting with actual episodes of transparent use. (pp. 12–13)
- **Graded Resource-Level Transparency** (single-source): Resource-level transparency varies in degree with the proportion of concurrent cognitive processes running through a resource that are transparent rather than opaque, even when each extended process satisfies strict non-manipulation transparency. (pp. 16–18)

## Propositions

- **Phenomenal Transparency Is Necessary for Cognitive Extension** (single-source; umbrella — *discusses; defends viability under process-relative sharpening*, pp. 1–4, 16–18): Phenomenal transparency of an external artifact is a necessary condition for cognitive extension.
- **Disappearance from Apprehension Is Necessary for Cognitive Extension** (single-source; *rejects*, pp. 10–12): Necessarily, every external artifact employed in perceptual cognitive extension disappears from conscious apprehension.
- **Process-Relative Vehicle Transparency Is Necessary for Cognitive Extension** (single-source; *discusses / leaves unimpeded*, pp. 14–18): Necessarily, every extended cognitive process employs its external resource without relying on conscious manipulation of that resource's vehicle properties.

## Arguments

### TVSS Self-Observation Refutes Disappearance-from-Apprehension Requirement *(pp. 10–12)*

1. Possibly, a cognitive scientist consciously attends to states of a tactile-visual sensory substitution device on a monitor while simultaneously perceiving distal objects through that device.
2. Necessarily: if a cognitive scientist consciously attends to states of a tactile-visual sensory substitution device on a monitor while simultaneously perceiving distal objects through that device, then some external artifact employed in perceptual cognitive extension does not disappear from conscious apprehension.
3. Possibly, some external artifact employed in perceptual cognitive extension does not disappear from conscious apprehension.
4. Therefore, it is not the case that necessarily, every external artifact employed in perceptual cognitive extension disappears from conscious apprehension.

### TVSS Self-Observation Defeats Conscious-Apprehension Objections *(pp. 6–18)*

1. Possibly, a cognitive scientist consciously attends to states of a tactile-visual sensory substitution device on a monitor while simultaneously perceiving distal objects through that device.
2. Necessarily: if a cognitive scientist consciously attends to states of a tactile-visual sensory substitution device on a monitor while simultaneously perceiving distal objects through that device, then some external artifact employed in perceptual cognitive extension does not disappear from conscious apprehension.
3. Both phenomenal transparency denotes process-relative employment of a cognitive resource without conscious manipulation of its vehicle properties and if (possibly, some external artifact employed in perceptual cognitive extension does not disappear from conscious apprehension), then conscious-apprehension objections fail against process-relative phenomenal transparency.
4. Possibly, some external artifact employed in perceptual cognitive extension does not disappear from conscious apprehension.
5. If (possibly, some external artifact employed in perceptual cognitive extension does not disappear from conscious apprehension), then conscious-apprehension objections fail against process-relative phenomenal transparency.
6. Therefore, conscious-apprehension objections fail against process-relative phenomenal transparency.
