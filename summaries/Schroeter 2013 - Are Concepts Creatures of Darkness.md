# Schroeter 2013 - Are Concepts Creatures of Darkness

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Schroeter-2013-Are Concepts Creatures of Darkness-Analytic Philosophy.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Schroeter-2013-Are Concepts Creatures of Darkness-Analytic Philosophy|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

In *Our Knowledge of the Internal World* (2008), Robert Stalnaker defends a radically externalist and contextualist theory of mental content, arguing that unstructured propositions (sets of possible worlds) fully suffice to explain cognitive significance phenomena without positing fine-grained representational states or Fregean senses. On Stalnaker's view, "concepts" are Cartesian "creatures of darkness" that encourage theoretical confusion. Against this dismissal, this article argues that there are compelling, non-Cartesian reasons for positing concepts within an externalist framework. Analyzing Stalnaker's contextualist treatments of Kripke's Pierre puzzle and Boghossian's Peter slow-switching puzzle reveals that Stalnaker's own metasemantic explanations implicitly depend on commonsense assumptions about stable cognitive organization—specifically, persisting standing attitudes bound by relations of apparent *de jure* co-reference. A minimalist conception of mental files captures the functional mechanisms underwriting these appearances at the person level without incurring internalist commitments to transparent semantic access. Furthermore, Stalnaker's semantics faces two deeper challenges: (1) radical contextualism yields truth-conditions that are too unstable and interest-relative to satisfy the core explanatory requirements of descriptive generality and normative belief revision, which push toward stable, context-neutral truth-conditions; and (2) possible-worlds propositions cannot directly represent the internal mind–mind relations structuring complex cognition, which structured propositions mirroring mental files naturally capture. Rather than being creatures of darkness, concepts provide a legitimate and necessary semantic tool for externalism.
Source: pp. 1–16 [pp. 277–292].

## Main Summary

### Stalnaker’s Externalism

In *Our Knowledge of the Internal World* (2008), Robert Stalnaker articulates a sophisticated defense of a radically externalist and contextualist approach to mental content, framed as an effort to eliminate the final remnants of Cartesian internalism (p. 1). Whereas Cartesianism begins with the assumption of transparent epistemic access to an inner realm of mental states and attempts to deduce knowledge of the external world from that foundation, externalism begins "in the middle of things," crediting physical science and treating thinkers as physical systems within the natural world (p. 1). For the externalist, content attribution explains mental states in terms of dispositions to act and respond to objective features of the environment (p. 1, n. 2). The externalist challenge is to explain the distinctive character of self-knowledge without positing privileged or transparent access to an inner mental sphere (p. 1).

Stalnaker addresses three primary aspects of mental states: the qualitative character of experience (*qualia*), self-locating information, and judgments of sameness and difference of mental contents (pp. 1–2). Throughout his treatment of mental content, Stalnaker maintains that unstructured propositions—modeled as sets of possible worlds—provide a complete semantic account of mental states (p. 2). He denies that cognitive significance phenomena require fine-grained representational vehicles or Fregean senses, concluding that there is no legitimate theoretical work for concepts to perform (p. 2). Indeed, Stalnaker characterizes concepts as "creatures of darkness" that foster philosophical confusion by embodying the mistaken Cartesian assumption of internal states providing transparent access to represented entities (p. 2). Schroeter disputes this anti-representationalist conclusion, arguing that positing concepts is motivated by explanatory demands entirely independent of Cartesian internalism, and that Stalnaker's own interpretive solutions to puzzles of mental content implicitly rely on the cognitive structures concepts characterize (p. 2).

### Stalnaker on Pierre and Peter

To test whether externalist semantics can account for a thinker's perspective on sameness and difference of content without concepts, Schroeter examines two classic puzzle cases: Kripke's Pierre and Boghossian's Peter (p. 2). Pierre acquires the French belief he expresses as '*Londres est jolie*' from travel pictures, and later, living in an unattractive London neighborhood without realizing it is the same city, acquires the belief he expresses as 'London is not pretty' (p. 2). If content is assigned purely *de re*, Pierre holds contradictory standing beliefs about London, even though he is intuitively innocent of any logical error (p. 2). In Boghossian's slow-switching case, Peter moves from Earth to Twin Earth without noticing; he combines a standing memory expressed as 'There was water in the lake ten years ago' (referring to H2O) with a fresh perceptual belief 'There is water in the lake now' (referring to XYZ), concluding that there was water in the lake at both times (pp. 2–3). Interpreted *de re*, Peter commits the fallacy of equivocation; yet intuitively, his reasoning appears logically valid (p. 3).

Standard accounts resolve these puzzles by appealing to concepts as "ways of thinking about a topic": Pierre avoids contradiction because his beliefs involve two distinct concepts of London, while Peter's reasoning is deductively valid because he deploys a single concept across premises (p. 3). Stalnaker rejects both the descriptivist and mental file elaborations of this conceptual strategy (p. 3, n. 5). Descriptivism, on Stalnaker's diagnosis, is compromised by its search for transparent epistemic foundations, while mental files are dismissed as subpersonal mechanisms belonging to cognitive science rather than ordinary, person-level content attribution (p. 3, n. 5).

Instead, Stalnaker offers an account grounded in two central commitments: radical contextualism, according to which content attributions are relative to an interpreter's explanatory interests with no context-neutral facts of content (p. 3), and a transparency constraint, which mandates that content attributions must depict rational subjects as having transparent access to sameness and difference of their thought contents (p. 3). When evaluating Pierre's childhood belief in isolation, an interpreter focusing on the city itself assigns a *de re* proposition (p. 4). However, when contrasting Pierre's two beliefs, an interpreter seeking to respect Pierre's rationality assigns descriptive, *de dicto* truth-conditions—such as that the city called '*Londres*' is pretty and the city called 'London' is not—by individuating possible worlds via qualitative properties rather than the individual *res* (p. 4, nn. 6–7). 

For Peter's water inference, Stalnaker similarly identifies two divergent contextual interpretations (pp. 4–5). If the interpreter focuses on Peter's deductive rationality, Peter is interpreted as thinking unambiguously about H2O throughout; his inference is logically valid, though his second premise is empirically false due to perceptual misidentification (p. 4). If the interpreter instead focuses on the diachronic preservation of Peter's standing beliefs from their formative environments, the premises are interpreted as *de re* propositions about H2O and XYZ, supplemented by a tacit background belief (reconstructed via diagonalization as a meta-representational belief) that a single substance is involved (pp. 4–5, n. 8). For Stalnaker, propositional attributions function as contextual "spot-checks" that isolate relevant truth-conditions without needing fine-grained conceptual vehicles (p. 5).

### Interpretation and Cognitive Structure

Schroeter argues that Stalnaker's contextualist strategy leaves unexplained why commonsense interpretation finds Pierre and Peter immediately rational rather than perplexing (pp. 5–6). Commonsense psychology relies on two fundamental structural assumptions (p. 6). First, thinkers possess standing attitudes with enduring individual histories: Pierre's '*Londres*' and 'London' beliefs are distinct persisting states formed at different times, a historical distinction of which Pierre himself can be consciously aware (p. 6). Second, certain standing attitudes are linked by stable relations of apparent sameness of topic: Pierre's diverse '*Londres*' beliefs strike him as obviously pertaining to the same entity, a cognitive link essential for tracking topics, accumulating information, and recognizing logical relations (p. 6).

Peter's inference highlights a crucial dimension of this organization: apparent *de jure* co-reference (pp. 7–8). When Peter reasons about water in the lake, his inference strikes him as valid not merely because of conjunction-introduction, but because it is subjectively incontrovertible from his object-level perspective that his premises share the same subject matter as a matter of logical necessity (p. 7, nn. 10–11). Apparent *de jure* co-reference is an immediate, non-inferential relation connecting standing attitudes over time and within deductive transitions (pp. 7–8).

Schroeter demonstrates that Stalnaker's own contextual interpretations covertly depend on this cognitive architecture (p. 8). Pierre is judged rational because his '*Londres*' and 'London' beliefs lack apparent *de jure* co-reference; Peter is judged rational because his water attitudes are linked by apparent *de jure* co-reference (p. 8). Moreover, the shifting contextual assignments Stalnaker proposes track which relations of apparent *de jure* co-reference are made salient: synchronic inferential links motivate treating Peter's inference as uniformly about H2O, whereas diachronic links to origin motivate split *de re* assignments (p. 8). Thus, Stalnaker's externalist metasemantics is already committed to a commonsense psychology of cognitive structure (p. 8).

### Mental Files

Schroeter defends the mental file metaphor as a fruitful functional characterization of the mechanisms underwriting apparent *de jure* co-reference (pp. 8–9). Drawing an analogy to a physical manila folder labeled 'John Perry', Schroeter notes that the physical file binds documents so that a user is immediately disposed to treat them as pertaining to a single topic without prior content-matching (p. 9). Analogously, mental files are person-level functional mechanisms that bind and segregate bodies of attitudes, disposing the thinker to treat bound thoughts as *de jure* co-referential immediately and non-comparatively (p. 9).

This functional model defuses Stalnaker's two primary objections (pp. 9–10). Against the objection that files are subpersonal cognitive-science posits irrelevant to commonsense interpretation, Schroeter counters that files are defined by person-level appearances and conscious dispositions of apparent sameness of topic, which directly govern commonsense content ascriptions (pp. 9–10). Against the charge of hidden Cartesian internalism, Schroeter insists that mental files trace fallible cognitive dispositions rather than establishing transparent access to objective referents (p. 10, n. 14). Thinkers can be mistaken about co-reference, as Peter is when conflating H2O and XYZ; files constrain reference attributions strongly but defeasibly (p. 10).

Consequently, mental files can be seamlessly integrated into Stalnaker's metasemantics (pp. 10–11). Stalnaker can acknowledge that interpretation is sensitive to mental filing structure while preserving semantic externalism and possible-worlds propositions, arriving at a position close to John Perry's contextualism with mental files or incorporating file indices into centered belief states (pp. 10–11, nn. 15–16).

### Two Challenges for Stalnaker’s Semantics

Moving beyond metasemantics, Schroeter presents two challenges to Stalnaker's semantic framework (p. 11).

The first challenge attacks radical contextualism's denial of stable, context-neutral semantic contents (pp. 11–13). Commonsense psychology demands stable truth-conditions to fulfill two indispensable theoretical roles: descriptive generality and normative belief revision (p. 12). Following Tyler Burge, Schroeter emphasizes that communication, inquiry, and interpersonal debate require a general-purpose, context-neutral framework for comparing thoughts across persons and times (p. 12). Furthermore, rational belief revision requires stable truth-conditions; if truth-conditions varied with an interpreter's shifting conversational interests, rational thinkers would be licensed to abandon standing beliefs based on transient contextual concerns, destabilizing epistemic agency (p. 12). Schroeter proposes that while context-relative truth-conditions capture aspects of a subject's complex *implicit understanding* (recognitional dispositions, taxonomic models, and heuristics), the *real* semantic contents of standing attitudes consist of context-neutral normative truth-conditions (pp. 12–13). Stalnaker's reply—that stable truth-conditions merely reflect one more contingent explanatory context—fails to appreciate the privileged normative standing of belief-revision standards (p. 13, n. 17).

The second challenge questions the expressive adequacy of unstructured propositions (pp. 13–16). While sets of possible worlds adequately characterize simple stimulus–response systems (such as an insect robot navigating environmental inputs), human cognition possesses an additional dimension of stable mind–mind relations mediated by mental files (pp. 13–14). Unstructured propositions cannot directly represent this internal relational structure (p. 14). Stalnaker attempts to compensate by overloading metasemantics with shifting contextual assignments, resulting in a fragmented "spot-check" picture analogous to the blind men inspecting an elephant (pp. 14–15). Each context isolates one aspect of the subject's cognitive state, but the framework cannot semantically unify the thinker's total system of attitudes and normative standards (p. 15, n. 19). By contrast, structured propositions mirroring mental files directly capture apparent *de jure* co-reference and provide an integrated representation of both representational and logical dimensions of thought (p. 15). Concepts, far from being creatures of darkness, are indispensable tools for a comprehensive externalist semantics (p. 16).
