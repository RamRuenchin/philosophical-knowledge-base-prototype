# Collins 2008 - Content Externalism and Brute Logical Error

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Collins-2008-Content externalism and brute logical error-Canadian Journal of Philosophy.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Collins-2008-Content externalism and brute logical error-Canadian Journal of Philosophy|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

Synthesized abstract: The paper evaluates the epistemological consequence of content externalism known as the Argument from Logical Norms. Because externalism entails the denial of authoritative discriminative self-knowledge (DASK), unwitting switchers between environments (such as Earth and Twin Earth) can make inferences that equivocate between twin concepts without introspective detectability, resulting in brute logical error—an error in the logical form of one's inferences that betokens no defect in logical skill or rational care. Collins investigates whether externalists can block premise 2 of the Argument from Logical Norms (that denying DASK allows brute logical error). He examines three prominent compatibilist strategies: (1) denying the possibility of twin concepts by claiming that past memory contents shift to match the present environment (Michael Tye) or broaden into disjunctive/umbrella concepts (Jane Heal); (2) positing anaphor-like content links, inferential intentions, or preservative memory constraints that guarantee univocity mid-inference (Stephen Schiffer, Tyler Burge); and (3) treating the flawed inference as an enthymeme with a tacit false identity premise (such as *that water is twater*), thereby reducing the flaw to an empirical/presuppositional mistake rather than an error in logical reasoning. Collins argues that all three blocking strategies fail: twin concepts are genuinely possible under a preservative model of memory; anaphoric linkages cannot eliminate the equivocation that occurs when independently formed beliefs are conjoined in thought; and attributing tacit identity premises or presuppositions fails to validate the deductive inference or eliminate the logical error. Consequently, content externalism does entail the genuine possibility of brute logical error. Collins concludes by exploring the dialectical alternatives, noting that externalists must either refashion our norms of rationality—perhaps adopting non-culpable blaming policies as suggested by Jessica Brown—or demonstrate that our epistemic environments possess sufficient stability to support a default, defeasible entitlement to introspective judgments of content identity.
Source: Synthesized from pp. 549-574.

## Main Summary

### I Introduction

Externalism regarding mental content maintains that the contents of an individual's mental states are determined not merely by intrinsic physical properties, but partly by relations to the physical and social environment (p. 549). While externalism is a metaphysical thesis, it exerts serious epistemological consequences. Philosophers have long debated whether externalism is compatible with authoritative, privileged introspective self-knowledge of our own occurrent thoughts, since an earthling and her molecular twin on Twin Earth (where XYZ replaces H2O) share identical introspective experiences despite thinking different contents (*water* vs. *twater*) (pp. 549-550).

Externalists have defended compatibilism through several strategies: reliabilist accounts where second-order self-attributions are determined by the same environmental factors as first-order thoughts (e.g., John Heil 1988), or accounts distinguishing knowledge of content *simpliciter* from comparative or discriminative knowledge of content (e.g., Kevin Falvey and Joseph Owens 1994) (p. 550). Just as Lois Lane knows who Superman is and knows who Clark Kent is without knowing that they are identical, a thinker may know what she is thinking without possessing the introspective power to discriminate that thought from other thoughts playing an identical psychological role (p. 550). Most externalists consequently concede that externalism is incompatible with authoritative discriminative self-knowledge (DASK): the principle that for any subject S and mental state tokens M1 and M2, S can determine via introspection whether M1 and M2 have the same or different contents (p. 551). In slow switching cases, difference of content is not transparent; conversely, in cases like Peter encountering tomatoes under different pronunciations or guises, sameness of content is not transparent (p. 551).

### II Externalism and Brute Logical Error

#### 1. Brute Logical Error

The formal validity of an inference typically requires that specific constituents share identical contents across premises; for example, *modus ponens* requires identity between the antecedent of the conditional premise and the second premise (p. 551). If contents diverge, equivocation occurs and the inference is invalid (p. 551). Because difference of content is not transparent under externalism, equivocation in putative deductive inferences will not always be detectable by introspection, even by an astute thinker scrutinizing the inference (pp. 551-552). 

Collins illustrates this through the case of Peter: Peter lived on Earth and believes he took swimming lessons in water as a child; he is unwittingly transported to Twin Earth, interacts with twater, and acquires the concept *twater* while continuing to use the word "water" (p. 551). Believing that twater is condensing on his glass, Peter infers that the same kind of stuff he swam in as a child is now condensing on his glass (p. 551). His inference is deductively invalid due to equivocation between *water* and *twater*, yet he cannot detect this invalidity introspectively (p. 551). This engenders the possibility of brute logical error: a logical mistake that betokens no carelessness, malfunction, or irrationality on the part of the thinker (pp. 551-552, citing Burge 1988, p. 657). Although the root error is introspective, the result is an undeniable breakdown in reasoning—inferring a false conclusion from all true premises (p. 552).

#### 2. The Argument from Logical Norms

Logical norms dictate that rational agents must avoid simple logical errors; if the most astute and careful reasoners cannot conform to these norms because their logical form is opaque to introspection, our conception of logical norms and rationality appears threatened (pp. 552-553). Collins formalizes this challenge, suggested by Paul Boghossian (1992, 1994), as the *Argument from Logical Norms*:
1. If Content Externalism is correct, then DASK is false.
2. If DASK is false, brute logical error is possible.
3. If brute logical error is possible, the norms of logic and rationality must be re-fashioned.
4. It is not the case that the norms of logic and rationality must be re-fashioned.
5. Content Externalism is incorrect (p. 553).

Boghossian points out that folk psychology relies on principles presupposing DASK, such as principles governing the rational alignment of beliefs, desires, and conditional intentions (pp. 553-554). Collins notes several possible externalist replies to this argument: treating rational norms as *ceteris paribus* laws with rare exceptions; accepting logical luck where unluckily switched reasoners are genuinely culpable; biting the bullet and refashioning norms; arguing that internalists also face counterexamples to DASK; or arguing (following Ted Warfield) that even if switching creates counterfactual errors, actual thinkers are not switched and retain discriminative self-knowledge (pp. 554-555). Collins sets these broader replies aside to focus specifically on premise 2: does externalism's rejection of DASK truly entail the possibility of brute logical error (p. 555)?

### III Attempts to Block the Argument From Logical Norms

Collins examines three main compatibilist strategies for blocking the entailment from the denial of DASK to brute logical error: (1) denying that twin concepts are possible; (2) positing anaphoric links or inferential intentions that guarantee univocity; and (3) treating the inference as an enthymeme with a tacit false premise (p. 555).

#### 1. The Possibility of Twin Concepts

The first blocking strategy argues that an unwitting traveler cannot possess distinct concepts between which she cannot introspectively discriminate (twin concepts) (p. 555). Some philosophers argue that past water-thoughts flip to twater-thoughts upon switching (Michael Tye 1998), while others argue that the concept broadens into a disjunctive or umbrella concept covering both substances (Jane Heal 1998) (p. 555). Collins argues that even if these views were correct, the epistemological cost for externalism would persist: failures to introspect *sameness* of content (as in Kripke's Paderewski or London/Londres cases) would still prevent subjects from recognizing valid inferences or eliminating false beliefs (pp. 556-557). While Jessica Brown (2004) suggests that a Fregean externalist might preserve transparency of sameness by individuating senses by potential differences in attitude, this sacrifices one of externalism's primary motivations—interpersonal sameness of content (pp. 556-557).

Furthermore, Collins directly refutes the arguments against twin concepts. Tye's view that memory contents float free and shift to reflect the subject's current environment makes past thoughts about water impossible to remember, turning them into mismemories (p. 558). Collins points out that a subject can mistakenly take himself to compare past and present substances without his concepts actually shifting (p. 558). Heal's broadening view misconstrues causal reference-fixing: initial baptisms fix natural kind terms; reference-fixing is not a revolving process that continuously expands whenever novel exemplars are encountered (pp. 558-559). Following Tyler Burge's (1993, 1998) account of preservative memory, the primary function of memory is to preserve past propositions and their justificational force over time via causal connections, without needing to re-discriminate or re-identify the past thought as an object of investigation (pp. 560-561). Preservative memory thus retains the Earth-acquired *water* concept alongside newly acquired Twin-Earth *twater* concepts, establishing the genuine possibility of twin concepts (pp. 560-561).

#### 2. ‘Anaphoric’ Content Links

The second blocking strategy concedes that twin concepts exist but posits a mental mechanism—such as anaphoric dependence, binding, or a standing inferential intention—that guarantees that multiple occurrences of a term in an inference share the same content (pp. 561-562). Stephen Schiffer (1992) suggested that Peter would gladly rephrase his premises as "I swam in water as a child and some of *it* is now condensing on my glass," binding subsequent occurrences to the first (p. 562). 

Collins refutes this via *reductio ad absurdum* (pp. 562-564). First, a subject's willingness to substitute pronouns merely shows that he believes his terms co-refer, not that they actually do (p. 562). Second, claiming that an inferential intention prevents equivocation proves far too much, as it would render ordinary careless equivocation impossible (p. 562). Most decisively, in deductive reasoning, premises typically originate as independently formed beliefs (e.g., a memory of childhood swimming in water, and a current perception of twater condensing) (pp. 562-563). If these independent beliefs are transformed into anaphorically linked premises (an application of Church's lambda-abstraction rule), the equivocation occurs precisely in that transition (p. 563). Transforming `<I swam in water>` and `<twater is condensing>` into `<water is such that I swam in it and it is now condensing>` is an invalid inferential step (p. 563). 

Collins also rejects Burge's (1998) variant, which claims that preservative memory holds the initial concept constant across the argument so that the reasoner applies the *water* concept to the current twater environment (pp. 564-565). As Brown (2004) observed, this makes thought content absurdly order-dependent and prevents switchers from employing current thoughts in reasoning with past beliefs (pp. 564-565). Moreover, tying concepts together to avoid equivocation merely trades a logical error (reasoning invalidly from true premises) for an empirical error (introducing a false premise), failing to advance the thinker's epistemic goals (p. 565).

#### 3. The Enthymeme Strategy

The third blocking strategy claims that Peter's inference is an enthymeme: a valid argument containing a tacit, suppressed premise (such as *that water is twater*, expressed by Peter as "Water is water") (p. 566). On this view, Peter's reasoning is deductively valid but unsound due to an empirical false belief, absolving him of any logical error (p. 566). Collins sets out three criteria for validating an argument via an enthymematic premise: (1) the subject must believe the premise; (2) the premise must actually figure in the reasoning; and (3) believing the premise must not itself betoken logical incompetence (p. 566).

Collins argues against Tye's claim that Peter cannot believe that water is twater: Peter systematically confuses the two substances and would assent to "water is water," so he can be said to believe the identity (pp. 566-569). However, condition (2) fails (p. 569). If pressed, an astute reasoner like Peter would insist that the identity premise is completely superfluous to the deductive validity of his inference (p. 569). In ordinary reasoning, thinkers trade on the identity of objects perceived and remembered over time without supplying explicit identity premises (pp. 569-570, citing John Campbell 1994). If every argument relying on content identity required a tacit identity premise, an infinite regress would ensue (p. 569). 

Burge (1998) suggests that even if the identity is not a premise, it is a mistaken *presupposition*, so that there is an error in presupposition rather than reasoning (p. 570). Collins counters that a mistaken presupposition of univocity *just is* an error about the logical form of the inference (p. 571). Unless all presuppositions are elevated to premises (which Burge himself rejects), an inference lacking univocal terms remains deductively invalid (p. 571). Attributing the presupposition explains why an astute thinker committed the error, but it does not validate the reasoning (p. 571).

### IV Conclusions

Collins concludes that content externalism genuinely entails the possibility of brute logical error (p. 571). All three compatibilist strategies fail to insulate externalism from this consequence (p. 571). The externalist must therefore confront the Argument from Logical Norms directly by determining how norms of rationality should be refashioned (pp. 571-572). 

One promising approach, suggested by Jessica Brown (2004), is to adopt an epistemic blaming policy that distinguishes culpable from non-culpable logical errors: a thinker who reasons invalidly due to externalist content shifts is blameless if she would insist that she intended and understood the terms univocally (p. 572). Rational agents can be expected to conform to logical norms only relative to the logical properties accessible to them (p. 572). Alternatively, externalists might argue that because our actual physical and social environments are largely stable, thinkers enjoy a default, defeasible epistemic entitlement to take their thoughts to be univocal without empirical verification, despite counterfactual switching possibilities (pp. 572-573). Collins considers this entitlement strategy promising but notes that a comprehensive defense remains to be provided (p. 573).
