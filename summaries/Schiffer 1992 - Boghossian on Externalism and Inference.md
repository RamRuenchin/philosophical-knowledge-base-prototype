# Schiffer 1992 - Boghossian on Externalism and Inference

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Schiffer-1992-Boghossian on externalism and inference-Philosophical Issues.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Schiffer-1992-Boghossian on externalism and inference-Philosophical Issues|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

This paper examines and challenges Paul Boghossian's thesis in 'Externalism and Inference' that content externalism, combined with the language-of-thought hypothesis, entails that deductive reasoning can be invalidated by introspectively undetectable equivocation, thereby motivating a level of narrow content. Schiffer critically evaluates Boghossian's twin-earth thought experiment (Peter and Pavarotti), arguing that externalism provides no plausible mechanism for unambiguous tokens of ambiguous Mentalese types. Using an actual-world analog involving confused encounters with identical twins (Ralph and Yolanda), Schiffer demonstrates that a thinker who intends to refer to the same individual across an inference either employs the same singular mode of presentation across both premises or relies on a confused dossier that fails to refer. In neither case do the premises equivocate between different referents, as evidenced by the thinker's disposition to restate the reasoning as a single conjoined premise. Schiffer further shows that in inferences where subsequent tokens are generated purely by deductive derivation without external causal intervention, those tokens inherit the semantic value of their inferential source. Finally, Schiffer rejects Boghossian's inference to the best explanation for narrow content, arguing that an agent's epistemic justification can be fully explained without positing narrow content by noting that the inference instantiates a deductively valid logical form guaranteed to succeed but for undetectable externalist contingencies.
Source: pp. 29–37.

## Main Summary

### The Problem of Mentalese Ambiguity (inferred)

Suppose thinking occurs in a language of thought (Mentalese). Boghossian argues that there can be ambiguous Mentalese expression types with unambiguous tokens, and that this allows deductive or practical inferences to be invalidated by undetectable equivocation, motivating an argument for narrow content (p. 29). Schiffer notes that extant theories of Mentalese content, such as Jerry Fodor's causal asymmetric dependency theory, rule out type ambiguity by treating multiple causal sources as producing disjunctive meaning (pp. 29–30). Moreover, while ambiguous public words are disambiguated by speaker communicative intentions, speaker intention cannot secure disambiguation in the neural language of thought (p. 30). Because Boghossian offers no positive theory of Mentalese ambiguity or token disambiguation, his case rests entirely on an example (p. 30).

### Boghossian's Externalist Equivocation Case (inferred)

Boghossian's case concerns Peter, who forms a memory belief about Luciano Pavarotti swimming in Lake Taupo on Earth, is unknowingly transported to Twin Earth, and acquires beliefs about Twin Pavarotti while retaining his Earth memory (p. 31). Boghossian claims that Peter reasons from true premises: (1) Pavarotti once swam in Lake Taupo, and (2) the singer I heard yesterday is Pavarotti, to conclude that the singer heard yesterday swam in Lake Taupo (p. 31). Boghossian argues this inference is invalid because the two tokens of 'Pavarotti' differ in reference, equivocating between Luciano and Twin Luciano through a fallacy Peter cannot detect (p. 31).

### The Yolanda Counter-Case and the Univocality of Inference (inferred)

Schiffer counters with an actual-world analog: Ralph teaches identical twins attending class on different days, unwittingly calls both 'Yolanda', and infers that the woman before him on Thursday asked a good question on Tuesday because Yolanda asked a good question on Tuesday and the woman before him is Yolanda (p. 32). Schiffer shows that across the three plausible interpretations of Ralph's first premise—a singular memory belief under a demonstrative mode of presentation, a confused memory referring to the Thursday twin, or a confused dossier failing to refer to either—the same mode of presentation governs the second premise because Ralph intends to speak of the same person (pp. 32–33). If Ralph had distinct modes in mind, he would require an explicit identity premise (pp. 33–34). Thus, the inference is never vitiated by equivocation (p. 34).

### Single-Premise Restatement and Inferential Tokens (inferred)

Applying this to Peter, Schiffer argues that Peter means the same by both tokens of 'Pavarotti' (p. 34). This univocality is confirmed by Peter's willingness to restate his reasoning as a single conjoined premise: 'Pavarotti once swam in Lake Taupo and is the singer I heard yesterday' (p. 35). Furthermore, where a concluding token is generated purely by deductive inference without new external causes, it inherits the semantic value of the premises (pp. 35–36).

### Epistemic Justification and the Rejection of Narrow Content (inferred)

Finally, Schiffer rejects Boghossian's argument for narrow content (pp. 36–37). Boghossian proposes that narrow content is required via inference to the best explanation to explain Peter's epistemic justification (pp. 36–37). Schiffer replies that no narrow content is needed: Peter's epistemic justification is explained simply by observing that his reasoning instantiates a logical form guaranteed to be valid but for undetectable externalist contingencies (p. 37).

### Contingent-Form Reply Against Narrow Content *(pp. 36–37)*

1. Peter's conclusion belief is produced by an inference instantiating a deductive logical form guaranteed to be valid absent undetectable externalist contingencies.
2. If Peter's conclusion belief is produced by an inference instantiating a deductive logical form guaranteed to be valid absent undetectable externalist contingencies, then wide content alone suffices for rationalizing psychological explanation.
3. If wide content alone suffices for rationalizing psychological explanation, then it is not the case that both rationalizing psychological explanation requires a non-wide intentional or syntactic level and it is not the case that wide content alone suffices for rationalizing psychological explanation.
4. Wide content alone suffices for rationalizing psychological explanation.
5. Therefore, it is not the case that both rationalizing psychological explanation requires a non-wide intentional or syntactic level and it is not the case that wide content alone suffices for rationalizing psychological explanation.
