# Goldberg 1999 - The Relevance of Discriminatory Knowledge of Content

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Goldberg-1999-The Relevance of Discriminatory Knowledge of Content-Pacific Philosophical Quarterly.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Goldberg-1999-The Relevance of Discriminatory Knowledge of Content-Pacific Philosophical Quarterly|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

Those interested in securing the compatibility of anti-individualism and introspective knowledge of content (henceforth ‘compatibilists’) typically make a distinction between knowledge of content proper (KC) and discriminatory knowledge of content (DKC). Following Falvey and Owens (1994), most compatibilists allow that anti-individualism is not compatible with introspective DKC, but maintain that nonetheless anti-individualism _is_ compatible with introspective KC. Though I have raised doubts about the compatibility of anti-individualism and introspective KC elsewhere (Goldberg, 1997 and forthcoming), here my aim is to suggest the philosophical relevance of DKC itself. My thesis is that there are cases in which a thinker’s failing to have DKC will affect the justification which she takes herself to have in drawing various inferences in the course of her reasoning, and so will affect that reasoning itself. After presenting illustrative examples and suggesting why anti-individualists themselves ought to acknowledge this point, I suggest that the examples indicate further work for anti-individualists: formulating what it takes to have DKC, and substantiating the view (widely held by anti-individualists) that antiindividualism’s implication that we (often) lack such knowledge is not to be taken as an important weakness of anti-individualism itself.

Source: p. 136.

## Main Summary

### 1. Background

Anti-individualism holds that some thought contents cannot be individuated solely by properties of the thinker considered apart from her social and physical environment. This generates an apparent tension with authoritative self-knowledge: if content depends on environmental facts a thinker may not know, it can seem that she cannot know what she is thinking. Goldberg accepts Burge’s claim that the self-referential structure of present-tense self-ascriptions secures correct, justified judgments that one is thinking a given thought, but he separates that achievement from knowing what content one is self-ascribing. Most compatibilist anti-individualists answer the tension by distinguishing knowledge of content proper (KC) from discriminatory knowledge of content (DKC). They concede that a thinker may be unable to discriminate her actual thought from counterfactual thoughts she would have had in a different environment, while maintaining that this is only a possible failure of DKC, not of KC. Goldberg brackets the disputed question whether such failures undermine KC. His more modest thesis is that failures of DKC can still affect practical and theoretical reasoning by changing which inferences a thinker takes herself to be justified in drawing. Source: pp. 136–138.

### 2. The Relevance of Introspective DKC: the example of Sally

Goldberg develops the thesis through Sally, a thinker whose environmentally acquired concepts make uncertainty about her own past content relevant to current inference. The example is designed to be acceptable even to compatibilists who deny that a lack of discrimination destroys KC. Source: pp. 138–147.

#### 2.1

Sally first learns “pragmatic” in an ordinary linguistic community, where it denotes a realistic attitude toward achieving one’s goals. She defers to that community’s norms and is reasonably treated as sharing its concept despite lacking an independent account of the concept’s application conditions. At college she encounters “pragmatism” in its philosophical sense and similarly defers to her professors. Given anti-individualism and the difference between the two uses, Goldberg assumes that Sally acquires two concepts associated with the same word-form while remaining unaware of the distinction. The case therefore focuses on compatibilist anti-individualists who accept this two-concept description but maintain that Sally nevertheless knows the contents of her “pragmatist”-thoughts in the KC sense. Goldberg argues that they must still recognize that her lack of DKC matters to reasoning. Source: pp. 138–140.

#### 2.2

Sally remembers the belief “No pragmatists have any principles,” but not whether she acquired it in her ordinary community or in the Philosophy Department. After learning that her idiolect associates two concepts with “pragmatist,” she also learns that Jones is a pragmatist in the philosophical sense. She now counts herself as believing both that no pragmatists have principles and that Jones is a pragmatist, yet she cannot determine without investigating the provenance of the remembered belief whether those contents entail that Jones has no principles. The failure does not presuppose that Sally lacks KC. It consists in her inability to tell whether the same concept occurs in the two premises, and it changes whether she takes herself to be justified in drawing the conclusion. Thus a lack of discriminatory and explicational knowledge can directly affect reasoning even if compatibilism about KC is granted. Source: pp. 140–141.

#### 2.3

An objection denies that Sally’s present utterance preserves the original belief-content once she knows that “pragmatist” may express either of two concepts; perhaps her present belief is only the metalinguistic belief that whatever she earlier meant was true. Goldberg argues that this reply is unavailable to compatibilists. Before Sally learns of the two senses, there is no reason to deny that her memory preserves the original content. Learning of the distinction later gives her a reason to wonder which content it is but does not by itself transform or destroy the preserved content. Compatibilists already insist in present-tense cases that inability to discriminate one content from another does not prevent a thinker from entertaining or knowing the actual content. Consistency requires the same stance toward past-tense, content-preserving memory. If Sally’s memory preserved the earlier belief before she learned of the ambiguity, her later discriminatory failure alone cannot establish that it ceased to do so. Source: pp. 141–143.

#### 2.4

Goldberg argues that Sally-style cases need not be rare. Content-preserving memory is common, and thinkers often forget the circumstances in which remembered beliefs were acquired. The prevalence of the third condition—reason to think that one word is associated with distinct contents—depends partly on how finely anti-individualists individuate concepts. Ludlow’s account of widespread “world-switching” makes such cases especially common because movements among linguistic communities can alter deference relations and concept acquisition. Source: pp. 144–146.

The reasoning-relevant failure also does not require two subjectively indistinguishable concepts. Sally may be able to explicate both concepts perfectly well yet fail to know which one occurred in a particular remembered belief because she has forgotten its provenance. Nor must she actually have been world-switched. It is enough that she reasonably suspects a change of linguistic community and therefore suspects a shift in content. Since that suspicion arises from the anti-individualist dependence of content on community, anti-individualism itself makes uncertainty about remembered content a possible constraint on inference. An anti-individualist may reject fine-grained concept individuation or accept this consequence and defend it independently, but either response requires a developed account of DKC and its role in reasoning. Source: pp. 146–147.

### 3. Boghossian on Anti-individualism and Inference

Boghossian argues that externalism threatens our *a priori* ability to recognize valid inference because undetected world-switching can produce equivocation. In his Peter example, memories about the earthly Pavarotti and later beliefs about Twin-Earth Pavarotti can lead to an apparently valid but equivocating inference. Goldberg’s argument resembles this one in linking switching and failures of content discrimination to reasoning, but it is deliberately weaker: it neither relies on a disputed conception of the *a priori* nor claims that anti-individualism necessarily produces invalid-yet-undetectable inferences. Consequently, Burge’s objection that Boghossian conflates what is *a priori* determinable with what is internally detectable does not address Goldberg’s thesis. Source: pp. 148–149.

Schiffer and Burge offer a second reply to Boghossian: a reasoner can intend repeated word-forms to be used univocally, so that the context of the argument fixes the same reference at each occurrence. This may ensure validity, but Goldberg argues that it does not solve Sally’s practical problem. Her goal is not merely to construct a valid argument; it is to decide truly whether Jones has no principles on the basis of contents delivered by memory. Stipulating a univocal use could preserve validity only by risking a false conclusion if the remembered premises originally expressed different concepts. DKC therefore remains relevant whenever reasoning aims at sound belief formation rather than formal validity alone. Burge partly recognizes this point by allowing that some reasoners tacitly and mistakenly presuppose that repeated expressions apply to the same objects. Goldberg contends that the point should be made explicit and generalized: even if anti-individualism avoids Boghossian’s charge, it still entails possible DKC failures that shape reasoning. Source: pp. 149–151.

### 4. The Significance of the Thesis

The KC/DKC distinction does not finish the anti-individualist response to incompatibilism. Even if failures of DKC leave KC intact, anti-individualists must explain why the comparative loss of DKC is not a serious weakness and must say what DKC consists in and how it relates to KC. If “knowledge of content” is meant to include the knowledge that enables thinkers to use thoughts as premises in arguments or practical syllogisms, a notion of KC that excludes DKC is insufficient. Appeals to the self-referential character of self-ascription may secure KC, but they do not yet provide a full theory of a thinker’s knowledge of her own content. Goldberg therefore presents the paper as a constructive agenda for anti-individualism: it should motivate and articulate an account of discriminatory knowledge of content adequate to reasoning. Source: p. 152.

## Relevant Literature

- [Burge 1988, “Individualism and Self-Knowledge”]
  Why relevant: Goldberg accepts Burge’s account of the self-referential basis of authoritative present-tense self-ascription while arguing that it does not settle the role of DKC.
- [Burge 1993, “Content Preservation”]
  Why relevant: Goldberg relies on Burge’s account of content-preserving memory to defend Sally’s retention of her earlier belief-content.
- [Burge 1998, “Memory and Self-Knowledge”]
  Why relevant: Goldberg uses Burge’s replies to switching and equivocation arguments as a compatibilist framework whose implications for reasoning need fuller articulation.
- [Boghossian 1992a, “Externalism and Inference”]
  Why relevant: Goldberg’s thesis is developed as a weaker, reasoning-focused successor to Boghossian’s argument about externalism, equivocation, and *a priori* logical competence.
- [Schiffer 1992, “Boghossian on Externalism and Inference”]
  Why relevant: Goldberg argues that Schiffer’s univocal-reference strategy addresses deductive validity but not the truth-directed practical reasoning at issue in Sally’s case.
- [Falvey and Owens 1994, “Externalism, Self-Knowledge, and Skepticism”]
  Why relevant: Goldberg treats their KC/DKC distinction as representative of the compatibilist strategy whose account of knowledge of content remains incomplete.
- [Ludlow 1995a, “Externalism, Self-Knowledge, and the Prevalence of Slow Switching”]
  Why relevant: Ludlow’s prevalence thesis supports Goldberg’s claim that Sally-style uncertainty about content may be widespread rather than exceptional.
