# Gerken 2011 - Conceptual Equivocation and Warrant by Reasoning

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Gerken-2011-Conceptual Equivocation and Warrant by Reasoning-Australasian Journal of Philosophy.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Gerken-2011-Conceptual Equivocation and Warrant by Reasoning-Australasian Journal of Philosophy|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

In this paper, I challenge a widely presupposed principle in the epistemology of inference. The principle, (Validity Requirement), is this: S’s (purportedly deductive) reasoning, R, from warranted premise-beliefs provides (conditional) warrant for S’s belief in its conclusion only if R is valid.

I argue against (Validity Requirement) from two prominent assumptions in the philosophy of mind: that the cognitive competencies that constitute reasoning are fallible, and that the attitudes operative in reasoning are antiindividualistically individuated. Indeed, my discussion will amount to a defence of anti-individualism against a novel ‘slow-switch’ argument against it.

This argument contra anti-individualism has it that given anti-individualism and certain auxiliary assumptions, A, a switched reasoner may, in certain slow-switch circumstances, C, reason invalidly by equivocating concepts. More specifically:

- (Valid 0): Peter is in circumstances C, and auxiliary assumptions, A, hold. (Valid 1): If Peter is in circumstances C, and auxiliary assumptions A hold, then (if the attitudes operative in Peter’s reasoning R are antiindividualistically individuated, then R is not valid).

(Valid 2): Peter’s reasoning, R, generates warrant for the conclusion-belief. (Valid 3): Peter’s reasoning, R, generates warrant for the conclusion-belief only if the reasoning, R, is valid.

- (Valid 4): So, the attitudes operative in Peter’s reasoning R are not antiindividualistically individuated.

The argument involves weaker premises than those of familiar slow-switch arguments against anti-individualism. In particular, it requires only that the reasoning be de facto valid. This assumption is much weaker than the requirement that the validity of the reasoning be ‘transparent’ to the reasoner. Indeed, (Valid 3) is simply an instance of (Validity Requirement).

However, I argue that anti-individualism and (Valid 0)–(Valid 2) should be upheld at the expense of (Valid 3). In consequence, (Validity Requirement) stands in need of restriction. Thus, I argue for a surprising result in the epistemology of inference from widely accepted assumptions in the philosophy of mind.
Source: p. 381 (PDF p. 2).

## Main Summary

### 1. Introduction

Gerken investigates epistemic warrant acquired through reasoning by integrating the epistemology of inference with the philosophy of mind along two axes: the cognitive competencies whose exercise constitutes reasoning, and the anti-individualistic individuation of the attitudes operative in reasoning (p. 381 / PDF p. 3). Warrant is treated as a genus encompassing internalist justification and externalist entitlement (p. 381 / PDF p. 3, n. 1). The paper pursues a five-stage argument: Section 2 motivates the traditional requirement that purportedly deductive reasoning must be valid to confer warrant; Section 3 introduces a novel slow-switch reductio of anti-individualism ('The Validity Argument'); Section 4 argues contra the Validity Requirement that slow-switched reasoners can acquire warrant from reasoning that commits conceptual equivocation; Section 5 defends this competence-based account against the charge that the reasoner is merely cognitively blameless; and Section 6 outlines methodological morals for the epistemology of inference (pp. 381–382 / PDF pp. 3–4).

### 2. The Traditional Conception of Warrant By Reasoning

A broad traditional doctrine, (Environmental Independency), holds that the warranting force of purportedly deductive reasoning is independent of the thinker's environmental relations, depending exclusively upon the form of the reasoning and premise warrant (p. 381 / PDF p. 3).

#### 2.1 The Classical Biconditional: (Transmission) and (Validity Requirement)

The traditional conception is formalized in the classical biconditional (CB): S's (purportedly deductive) reasoning R from warranted premise-beliefs provides (conditional) warrant for its conclusion iff R is valid (p. 381 / PDF p. 3). While its right-to-left direction, (Transmission), is widely contested, its left-to-right direction, (Validity Requirement), has remained largely unchallenged (pp. 381–382 / PDF pp. 3–4).

Gerken clarifies three features of (Validity Requirement). First, 'purportedly deductive' is a normative classification: reasoning is purportedly deductive if it falls under the standard of truth-preservation in virtue of form, regardless of whether the thinker possesses the concept of deduction or forms higher-order representations of the inference (p. 382 / PDF p. 4). An invalid inference remains purportedly deductive if it is subject to formal truth-preservation standards and fails them (p. 382 / PDF p. 4). Second, the qualification '(conditional)' accommodates Harman's point that deductive logic specifies no rules of belief acceptance: finding an implausible conclusion may justify abandoning a premise rather than accepting the conclusion (pp. 382–383 / PDF pp. 4–5). Third, traditional theorists treat validity as strictly necessary for rationality, as seen in Bach's equation of rationality with logic, Goldfarb's definition of logical rules as correct reasoning, and Boghossian's thesis that the norms of rationality are the norms of logic (p. 383 / PDF p. 5).

#### 2.2 A Rationale for (Validity Requirement)

A principled rationale for (Validity Requirement) begins from the core epistemic demand that warranted belief requires a non-lucky connection to truth (pp. 383–384 / PDF pp. 5–6). Valid deductive inference contributes to warrant by being truth-preserving in virtue of form (p. 384 / PDF p. 6). If an invalid deductive inference happens to yield a true conclusion from warranted premises, that truth is epistemically lucky in the extreme and cannot confer warrant, motivating the slogan: 'No truth-preservation, no non-lucky truth-connection. No non-lucky truth-connection, no warrant' (p. 384 / PDF p. 6). This fits (Environmental Independency) and is abductively reinforced by the apparent absence of counterexamples (pp. 384–385 / PDF pp. 6–7).

#### 2.3 (Validity Requirement): Concluding Remarks

Although a single principle cannot capture an entire tradition, (Validity Requirement) expresses the core traditional view that deductive inferential warrant is constitutively tied to formal validity (p. 385 / PDF p. 7).

### 3. A Novel Slow-Switch Reductio of Anti-Individualism

Gerken develops a novel slow-switch argument against anti-individualism to test (Validity Requirement) (p. 385 / PDF p. 7).

#### 3.1 Two Working Hypotheses: Anti-Individualism and Well-Functioning Fallibility

Gerken adopts two working hypotheses. First, anti-individualism: mental attitudes are partly individuated by environmental relations that need not be introspectively available to the thinker, as illustrated by Ali (on Earth with aluminum) and twin-Ali (on Twin Earth with look-alike twaluminum), who possess distinct concepts despite identical internal physiology (pp. 385–386 / PDF pp. 7–8). Second, well-functioning fallibility: cognitive competencies can fail to achieve their constitutive function even when performing optimally (p. 386 / PDF p. 8). Specifically, Gerken introduces the 'univocality-competence'—the reasoning competence that reliably redeploys only univocal concepts as middle terms (p. 386 / PDF p. 8). Optimal exercise of this competence can fail when environmental shifts alter the concepts operated upon (p. 386 / PDF p. 8).

#### 3.2 Slow-Switching and Conceptual Equivocation

In Gerken's slow-switch scenario, Peter acquires beliefs on Earth about apples ($apple^e$), such as (Apple 1) 'Some $apples^e$ are red' (p. 387 / PDF p. 9). Unbeknownst to him, Peter is moved to Twin Earth, interacts with look-alike twin-apples ($apple^{te}$), and forms (Apple 2) 'All $apples^{te}$ are sweet' (p. 387 / PDF p. 9). When Peter infers (Apple 3) 'Something is both red and sweet', $apple^e$ and $apple^{te}$ operate as non-univocal middle terms; Peter's reasoning commits the fallacy of conceptual equivocation and is invalid (p. 387 / PDF p. 9).

While anti-individualism does not inherently entail equivocation—since alternative psychological paths include concept retention (Moya), memory replacement (Ludlow, Tye), anaphoric univocality preservation (Burge, Schiffer), amalgamation (Heal), or tacit identity beliefs (Burge)—equivocation is a genuine psychological possibility under specific circumstances C and auxiliary assumptions A (pp. 387–388 / PDF pp. 9–10). This yields premise (Valid 0)—that Peter is in C and A holds—and premise (Valid 1)—that if Peter is in C and A holds, anti-individualism entails that his reasoning R is invalid (pp. 388–389 / PDF pp. 10–11).

#### 3.3 The Validity Argument

The individualist can thus formulate The Validity Argument: (Valid 0) Peter is in C and A holds; (Valid 1) If Peter is in C and A holds, then if attitudes are anti-individualistically individuated, R is invalid; (Valid 2) Peter's reasoning R generates warrant for the conclusion-belief; (Valid 3) Peter's reasoning R generates warrant for the conclusion-belief only if R is valid; therefore, (Valid 4) The attitudes operative in Peter's reasoning R are not anti-individualistically individuated (p. 389 / PDF p. 11).

Crucially, premise (Valid 3) requires only de facto validity rather than introspective transparency or a priori knowability of validity, making it weaker than Boghossian's transparency premise and immune to standard anti-transparency objections (pp. 389–390 / PDF pp. 11–12). Because (Valid 3) is an instance of (Validity Requirement), the individualist reaches an internalist conclusion from widely accepted epistemic premises (p. 390 / PDF p. 12).

### 4. Warrant Without Validity

Gerken resolves the reductio by accepting anti-individualism and premises (Valid 0)–(Valid 2) while rejecting (Valid 3), thereby restricting (Validity Requirement) (pp. 390–391 / PDF pp. 12–13).

#### 4.1 Reasoning Competencies and Validity-Conduciveness

Within a competence-based epistemology, warrant depends on whether a belief is produced by cognitive competencies that are truth-conducive in epistemically normal circumstances, rather than on de facto success in a specific instance (pp. 391–392 / PDF pp. 13–14). For deductive reasoning, the relevant epistemic property is validity-conduciveness: the competence reliably preserves truth in virtue of form in normal environments (pp. 391–392 / PDF pp. 13–14). Peter's univocality-competence performs optimally, but abnormal environmental switching tricks it by altering its conceptual inputs (p. 392 / PDF p. 14). Because thinkers may rationally ignore abnormal possibilities absent defeating evidence, Peter's reasoning confers conditional warrant despite its de facto invalidity (pp. 392–393 / PDF pp. 14–15).

#### 4.2 Enter Pedro

Comparing Peter to his unswitched Earthian twin, Pedro, reinforces this result (p. 393 / PDF p. 15). Pedro carries out the same inference on Earth, validly reaching knowledge (p. 393 / PDF p. 15). Pedro is entitled to ignore the abnormal slow-switch possibility; because an abnormal scenario remains abnormal even when actual, Peter is equally entitled to ignore it (pp. 393–394 / PDF pp. 15–16). Peter and Pedro exercise type-identical reasoning competencies; Peter simply suffers environmental bad luck (p. 394 / PDF p. 16). Consequently, both are equally warranted in their conclusion-beliefs (p. 394 / PDF p. 16).

#### 4.3 Enter Gettier

The Peter/Pedro comparison parallels Gettier cases (p. 394 / PDF p. 16). In classic Gettier cases, Mr. Smith (who infers a true conclusion from a warranted false premise) and Mr. Doe (who forms a true belief from a sheep-shaped rock) possess warrant without knowledge, whereas their counterparts in normal environments (Mrs. Smith and Mrs. Doe) achieve knowledge (pp. 394–395 / PDF pp. 16–17). Abnormal circumstances defeat the fourth condition on knowledge while leaving epistemic warrant intact (p. 395 / PDF p. 17). Peter's true conclusion-belief is likewise too lucky to constitute knowledge, but his warrant is fully preserved (p. 395 / PDF p. 17).

#### 4.5 Concluding Remark

The Validity Argument is not a reductio of anti-individualism, but shows that (Validity Requirement) is overly restrictive and must be qualified (p. 395 / PDF p. 17).

### 5. A Pre-emptive Clarification

Gerken addresses the objection that Peter is merely cognitively blameless rather than epistemically warranted (p. 396 / PDF p. 18).

#### 5.1 The Charge of Deontologism

Critics (such as Brown and Goldberg) allege that defending (Valid 2) conflates warrant with mere blamelessness or deontological excuse (p. 396 / PDF p. 18). Gerken agrees that blamelessness is necessary for warrant, but denies that it is sufficient: 'Warrant is more than mere relief from blamelessness' (p. 396 / PDF p. 18).

#### 5.2 Epistemic Deontologism Eschewed

To vindicate externalist warrant, Gerken introduces Pete, a member of a community where everyone accepts and reasons by the gambler's fallacy with expert theoretical backing (pp. 396–397 / PDF pp. 18–19). Pete is cognitively blameless, yet his conclusion-beliefs lack warrant because his inference rules are objectively unreliable (p. 397 / PDF p. 19). The difference between Pete and Peter lies in the objective truth-conduciveness of their competencies: Peter exercises competencies that are highly validity-conducive in normal circumstances and fail only through abnormal external distortion, whereas Pete exercises inherently defective rules (pp. 397–398 / PDF pp. 19–20).

### 6. Conclusion

Epistemic warrant depends on the truth-conduciveness of cognitive competencies in normal circumstances rather than de facto outcomes (p. 398 / PDF p. 20). Deductive reasoning requires that underlying competencies be validity-conducive in normal environments, not that every instance be de facto valid (p. 398 / PDF p. 20). The traditional (Validity Requirement) must be restricted, showing that epistemology cannot evaluate inferential warrant in abstraction from the fallible cognitive competencies that constitute reasoning (p. 398 / PDF p. 20).

## Arguments

### Gerken Validity Argument Against Anti-Individualism *(pp. 385–395)*

1. Possibly, a slow-switched thinker performs a subjectively valid syllogism combining a pre-switch memory premise with a post-switch experiential premise.
2. If a slow-switched thinker performs a subjectively valid syllogism combining a pre-switch memory premise with a post-switch experiential premise, then that thinker commits an introspectively undetectable equivocation between distinct wide contents.
3. If both (both (both some mental states have contents that constitutively depend on external environmental factors and slow switching produces two-concept cohabitation with originary memory fixation) and (possibly, a slow-switched thinker performs a subjectively valid syllogism combining a pre-switch memory premise with a post-switch experiential premise)) and (if a slow-switched thinker performs a subjectively valid syllogism combining a pre-switch memory premise with a post-switch experiential premise, then that thinker commits an introspectively undetectable equivocation between distinct wide contents), then some purportedly deductive inference that generates conditional epistemic warrant commits an equivocation between distinct wide contents.
4. No purportedly deductive inference that generates conditional epistemic warrant commits an equivocation between distinct wide contents.
5. It is not the case that some purportedly deductive inference that generates conditional epistemic warrant commits an equivocation between distinct wide contents.
6. It is not the case that both (both (both some mental states have contents that constitutively depend on external environmental factors and slow switching produces two-concept cohabitation with originary memory fixation) and (possibly, a slow-switched thinker performs a subjectively valid syllogism combining a pre-switch memory premise with a post-switch experiential premise)) and (if a slow-switched thinker performs a subjectively valid syllogism combining a pre-switch memory premise with a post-switch experiential premise, then that thinker commits an introspectively undetectable equivocation between distinct wide contents).
7. It is not the case that both (both some mental states have contents that constitutively depend on external environmental factors and slow switching produces two-concept cohabitation with originary memory fixation) and (possibly, a slow-switched thinker performs a subjectively valid syllogism combining a pre-switch memory premise with a post-switch experiential premise).
8. Therefore, it is not the case that both some mental states have contents that constitutively depend on external environmental factors and slow switching produces two-concept cohabitation with originary memory fixation.

### Gambler Fallacy Refutation of Epistemic Deontologism *(pp. 396–398)*

1. Possibly, a reasoner commits the gambler's fallacy blamelessly due to non-culpable cognitive limitations and expert-backed community norms.
2. Necessarily: if a reasoner commits the gambler's fallacy blamelessly due to non-culpable cognitive limitations and expert-backed community norms, then some cognitively blameless deductive reasoner is not inferentially warranted.
3. Possibly, some cognitively blameless deductive reasoner is not inferentially warranted.
4. Therefore, it is not the case that necessarily, every cognitively blameless deductive reasoner is inferentially warranted.
