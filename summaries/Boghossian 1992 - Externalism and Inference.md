# Boghossian 1992 - Externalism and Inference

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Boghossian-1992-Externalism and inference-Philosophical Issues.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Boghossian-1992-Externalism and inference-Philosophical Issues|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

This paper investigates whether content externalism—the view that mental contents are individuated in part by relations to the physical and social environment—threatens authoritative first-person knowledge of thought content and the traditional thesis that thinkers can judge the logical validity of their inferences a priori. Boghossian argues that contemporary attempts to reconcile externalism with first-person authority establish only a thin "content infallibility" (the reliable self-attribution of single thought tokens) while failing to secure the epistemic transparency of content (the ability to know reflectively whether two thought tokens share or differ in meaning). By analyzing one-world traveling cases involving slow switching between Earth and Twin Earth, Boghossian demonstrates that externalism generates a neglected converse failure of transparency: two mentalese tokens of the identical syntactic type may carry distinct semantic values without the thinker being able to detect the difference. Because memories retain their original Earthly reference while present-environment beliefs switch to twin entities, a thinker's deductive and practical inferences can combine true premises that unwittingly equivocate on a term, producing a false conclusion from an inference that appears blindingly valid from the inside. Consequently, externalism is inconsistent with the a priority of logical abilities. Finally, because the traveler's reasoning genuinely rationalizes their actions from their own point of view, Boghossian concludes that preserving rationalizing explanation requires positing a non-wide level of intentional description.
Source: pp. 11-28.

## Main Summary

### 1 Introduction

Recent work in mental content theory has persuaded philosophers that propositional attitudes cannot be individuated individualistically, without regard to the physical or social environment (p. 11). While a traditional Cartesian view allowed de re attitudes to vary externally while internal states remained fixed, de dicto attitudes were held to depend wholly on internal facts (pp. 11-12). Externalism rejects this internalist constraint, maintaining that commonly ascribed de dicto attitudes are partly individuated by external facts (p. 12). Foundational thought experiments support this: Putnam’s Twin Earth case (water vs. twater); Burge’s social externalism (arthritis vs. tharthritis); and Davidson’s Swampman case, where a duplicate lacking causal history lacks thought content altogether (pp. 12-13). Boghossian investigates whether externalism threatens authoritative first-person knowledge of thought content (p. 13). He defends two principal theses: first, externalism is inconsistent with the epistemic transparency of thought content; second, this failure undermines the traditional thesis that thinkers can determine a priori whether their inferences are logically valid (p. 13).

### 2 Reconciling Content-Infallibility with Externalism

Many philosophers deny any conflict between externalism and first-person content knowledge (p. 13). Boghossian examines a prominent reconciliation strategy articulated by John Heil and endorsed by Donald Davidson, Tyler Burge, and Barry Loewer and Ernest LePore (pp. 13-14). Heil argues that if first-order state M has content p fixed by external state A, and introspective state M' has content fixed by state A', nothing prevents A' from causally endowing M' with content encompassing M (pp. 13-14). In a language-of-thought framework, believing p consists in tokening sentence S (meaning p) in one's belief box (p. 14). Heil's model relies on syntactic mechanisms ensuring that whenever S enters the belief box, 'I believe S' enters as well; provided embedded and unembedded tokens of S express identical content, the second-order belief reliably reports the first-order state (p. 15). Externalism thus appears compatible with content infallibility (p. 15).

### 3 Self-Knowledge and Content Transparency

Boghossian argues that Heil-style reconciliation is hollow: it secures at best reliable true belief rather than justified knowledge, and fails to deliver the epistemic transparency of content (pp. 15-16). Adopting Michael Dummett’s principle that meaning is transparent—so that anyone attaching meaning to two words must know upon reflection whether their meanings are identical—Boghossian observes that knowing the content of S and of synonymous S' does not imply knowing they are synonymous, nor does it guarantee knowing whether distinct tokens of S share the same meaning (pp. 15-16). Existing literature documents transparency failure where distinct syntactic types share identical meaning without the thinker knowing it ([A]) (pp. 16-17). Boghossian points out that externalism equally entails the converse failure ([B]): two tokens of the same syntactic type may have different meanings without the thinker being able to detect the difference (p. 17). This converse failure directly threatens the a priority of logical abilities (p. 17).

### 4 The Semantics of Travel

To demonstrate converse transparency failure ([B]), Boghossian analyzes 'one-world traveling cases' involving slow switching (p. 18). Suppose Earthling Peter is unwittingly transported to Twin Earth, living there without detecting mental disruption (p. 18). Externalists agree that while quick switching does not alter thoughts, prolonged residence causes Peter's mentalese token 'water' to switch from meaning water to meaning twater (p. 18). Under externalist principles, content is determined by the environmental property that typically causes and sustains perceptions of that type (pp. 18-19). Crucially, thoughts whose sustaining causal origin remains Earthly retain Earthly interpretations even on Twin Earth (p. 19). Memories and past-directed beliefs are caused and sustained by past perceptions and owe nothing to current environmental interactions (p. 19). For example, Peter's memory of seeing Luciano Pavarotti swimming in Lake Taupo is sustained by past perceptions of Pavarotti and Lake Taupo; even after moving to Twin Earth and using 'Pavarotti' for Twin Pavarotti in present beliefs, his memories of the Taupo encounter continue to refer to Earthly Pavarotti and Earthly water (pp. 20-21).

### 5 Externalism and the a priority of our Logical Abilities

Because Peter's memories retain Earthly reference while present attitudes refer to Twin entities, his mentalese contains tokens of the identical syntactic type ('Pavarotti', 'Lake Taupo', 'water') that carry different semantic values without introspective indication of ambiguity (p. 21). This breaks down the relation between formal derivability and logical validity (pp. 21-22). Peter combines an Earthly memory with a recent Twin-Earth concert experience in a deductive inference: Pavarotti once swam in Lake Taupo (Earthly memory); the singer I heard yesterday is Pavarotti (Twin-Earth memory); therefore, the singer I heard yesterday once swam in Lake Taupo (pp. 21-22). From Peter's internal perspective, this inference appears blindingly valid; yet it is logically invalid because it equivocates on 'Pavarotti', leading from true premises to a false conclusion (pp. 21-22). Analogous failures occur in practical reasoning: Peter's present desire ('I want to embrace anyone who once swam in Lake Taupo') and Earthly memory ('Pavarotti once swam in Lake Taupo') yield an intention ('I shall set off to embrace Pavarotti') that fails to follow from his premises (p. 22). Thus, externalism undermines a thinker's ability to judge a priori whether an inference satisfies a valid logical form (pp. 22-23).

### 6 Peter and Pierre

Boghossian clarifies Peter's case by contrasting it with Saul Kripke’s puzzle about Pierre (p. 23). Pierre has two tokens of distinct syntactic types ('Londres' and 'London') sharing the same referent without realizing it, whereas Peter has two tokens of the same syntactic type ('Pavarotti' and 'Pavarotti') differing in referent without realizing it (p. 23). While Kripke used Pierre to argue that ordinary belief attribution breaks down, Boghossian notes that Kripke's argument crucially assumes externalism: Kripke grants that non-natural kind terms ('happy', 'doctor') have transparent synonymy because their meanings are internally determined, whereas natural kind terms ('rabbit', 'beech') lack transparent synonymy because reference is fixed by external causal facts (pp. 24-26). Crucially, Pierre is not logically deficient: believing 'Londres is pretty' and 'London is not pretty' does not constitute believing a formal contradiction (P and not-P) (p. 26). By contrast, Peter performs an actual inference whose apparent validity is destroyed by undetected semantic equivocation, demonstrating that externalism is inconsistent with a priori logical competence (p. 26).

### 7 The a priority of our Logical Abilities and the idea of Rationalization

Boghossian investigates why the a priority of logic is needed, arguing that it plays a fundamental role in fixing what it is for propositional attitudes to provide rationalizing explanations of action (p. 26). A rationalizing explanation shows why an action made sense from the agent's internal perspective in light of their beliefs and desires (pp. 26-27). When Peter infers an intention to embrace Twin Pavarotti, his reasons genuinely rationalize his intention from his point of view; it is wrong to claim he has no reason for his action (p. 27). Furthermore, in a counterfactual world where Peter lived identically without transport to Twin Earth, his inference would be fully valid; yet counterfactual Peter and traveler Peter do not differ in their capacity to reason (p. 27). If confined to wide contents, Peter's premises refer to distinct entities, rendering his inference an equivocation that fails to rationalize his behavior (pp. 27-28). Boghossian concludes that because traveler Peter's behavior genuinely makes sense from his point of view, this provides an argument for a distinct level of intentional description (such as narrow content) that preserves rationalizing explanation (p. 28).
