# Faria 2009 - Unsafe reasoning A survey

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Faria-2009-Unsafe Reasoning A Survey-Dois Pontos.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Faria-2009-Unsafe Reasoning A Survey-Dois Pontos|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

Judgments about the validity of at least some elementary inferential patterns (say _modus ponens_ ) are _a priori_ if anything is. Yet a number of empirical conditions must in each case be satisfied in order for a particular inference to instantiate this or that inferential pattern. We may on occasion be entitled to presuppose that such conditions are satisfied (and the entitlement may even be _a priori_ ), yet only experience could tell us that such was indeed the case. Current discussion about a perceived incompatibility between content externalism and first-person authority exemplifies how damaging the neglect of such empirical presuppositions of correct reasoning can be. An externalistic view of mental content is ostensibly incompatible with the assumption that a rational subject should be able to avoid inconsistency no matter what the state of her empirical knowledge may be. That fact, however, needs not be taken (as it often is) as a _reductio_ of externalism: alternatively, we may reject that assumption, adding to the agenda of a philosophical investigation of rationality an examination of the vicissitudes of logical luck. I offer an illustration and defense of that alternative.
Source: PDF p. 1.

## Main Summary

### From moral luck to logical luck (inferred)

Faria begins from the familiar thought that responsibility requires control, then uses the debate over moral luck to complicate it. A reckless driver may lack actual control over whether a child happens to cross the road, yet negligence still matters because the result could have been avoided in nearby circumstances through conduct within the agent's control. Faria is not primarily concerned with moral luck, but with Roy Sorensen's analogous notion of "logical luck": whether a subject reasons correctly may depend on empirical conditions that are not fixed by her grasp of an inference rule. His announced aim is to use this comparison to illuminate content externalism and to distinguish excusable from inexcusable ignorance, a distinction obscured by standard slow-switching cases. Source: PDF pp. 1-3.

### Empirical presuppositions of valid inference (inferred)

Elementary inferences depend on more than abstract logical form. From "this paperweight is a stone from Walden Pond" one may infer that something is such a stone only if the demonstrative refers; from "everything must perish" one may infer that Vanina must perish only if the name denotes something in the relevant domain. Likewise, combining "Laura is Canadian" and "Laura is blonde" requires the two tokens of "Laura" to corefer. These conditions concern existence, uniqueness, persistence, and identity, and their satisfaction is contingent and empirically defeasible. Thus logical competence may establish that a form is valid without establishing that a concrete piece of reasoning instantiates that form. Source: PDF pp. 3-5.

The Golden Retriever case makes the point vivid. Faria encounters a friendly dog and later a restless dog, then infers that some neighborhood dog is both friendly and restless. If the encounters involve Harry and Barry, two similar dogs, the reasoning is not a valid conjunction-and-existential inference but an equivocation that the reasoner mistakenly classifies as valid. Treating the assumed identity of the dogs as a suppressed premise does not solve the problem, because the tokens within that identity claim also require coreference. Requiring further identity premises generates a Carroll-style regress. Empirical presuppositions therefore enable the application of logical rules without simply entering the inference as additional premises. Source: PDF pp. 4-5, 12-13.

### Slow switching and Boghossian's challenge (inferred)

Putnamian and Burgean thought experiments stipulate pairs of environments that are indiscernible from the subject's first-person perspective. In Burge's slow-switching scenario, a subject alternates between Earth and Twin Earth without noticing, although environmental changes alter the contents expressed by her terms. Boghossian argues that externalism consequently permits undetectable invalidity: after a switch, a subject may combine a memory about playing in H2O-water with a current perception of XYZ-water and conclude falsely that the current glass contains the liquid of her childhood. If content shifts cannot be detected *a priori*, externalism appears to conflict with the traditional claim that rational subjects can detect the validity of their own inferences *a priori*. Source: PDF pp. 6-9.

Faria stresses that the cases build the subject's ignorance into their description. The experimenter stipulates what the protagonist cannot know, and the worlds contain no accessible trace of the switch. It is therefore unsurprising that both internalists and externalists seek to exculpate the subject. Internalists appeal to narrow content that preserves what makes sense from the subject's perspective. Schiffer and Burge treat later uses as anaphorically dependent on preserved earlier content, turning the apparent equivocation into a valid argument with a false current premise. Ludlow's Orwellian view instead interprets memory relative to present environmental conditions, again yielding validity at the cost of a false premise. Source: PDF pp. 9-11.

### Excusable and inexcusable ignorance (inferred)

These exculpating strategies share an assumption Faria attributes explicitly to Sosa: ignorance should not itself make an inferring subject incoherent, since the subject ought in principle to be able to avoid invalidity whatever she knows. Faria replies that slow-switching fantasies make ignorance look uniformly excusable by eliminating every epistemically accessible standpoint from which it could be corrected. Ordinary reasoning is different. A person who knows that a housemaid often replaces glasses, or that a neighbor breeds very similar dogs, neglects available evidence if she assumes object identity across occasions. Likewise, combining tensed judgments while ignoring their different times disregards familiar facts about their truth conditions. Source: PDF pp. 11-12.

Reasoning about changing objects is consequently "unsafe": it unavoidably relies on risky assumptions about existence, uniqueness, continuity, and coreference. Accountability should turn not on whether the subject actually controlled all relevant facts, but on whether the missing information would have been available in sufficiently close and epistemically accessible possibilities had the subject taken reasonable care. Logical luck is therefore compatible with differentiated responsibility. Some invalidity rests on genuinely inaccessible conditions and is excusable; other invalidity reflects negligence or inexcusable ignorance. Faria closes by urging epistemology to shift from the abstract question of whether knowledge is possible toward the practical question of what competent adults can reasonably be expected to know and how their claims answer demands for justification. Source: PDF pp. 12-14.

  Why relevant: Sosa states the assumption that ignorance alone is insufficient for incoherence, which Faria contests.

## Arguments

- [[Slow-Switch Equivocation vs. Token-Wide APLA]] (`ARG-03`) — rejects anti-externalist conclusion by abandoning token-wide `APLA` (`P-21`) in favor of logical luck (pp. 1, 6–12)

### Carroll-Style Regress Against Treating Empirical Coreference Presuppositions as Premises *(pp. 4–5, 12–13)*

1. Every ordinary finite inference that instantiates a valid logical form is completed in finitely many premises.
2. If some empirical coreference presupposition required for a concrete inference to instantiate a valid logical form can be converted into an explicit identity premise of that inference, then completing that inference requires an infinite sequence of further identity premises for the tokens in each added identity premise.
3. If completing that inference requires an infinite sequence of further identity premises for the tokens in each added identity premise, then it is not the case that every ordinary finite inference that instantiates a valid logical form is completed in finitely many premises.
4. If some empirical coreference presupposition required for a concrete inference to instantiate a valid logical form can be converted into an explicit identity premise of that inference, then it is not the case that every ordinary finite inference that instantiates a valid logical form is completed in finitely many premises.
5. It is not the case that some empirical coreference presupposition required for a concrete inference to instantiate a valid logical form can be converted into an explicit identity premise of that inference.
6. Therefore, no empirical coreference presupposition required for a concrete inference to instantiate a valid logical form can be converted into an explicit identity premise of that inference.

### Unsafe Reasoning and Environmental Logical Luck *(pp. 1–5, 12–14)*

1. Concrete inferences depend on contingent empirical presuppositions of existence, uniqueness, persistence, and coreference that are not fixed by abstract logical competence.
2. If concrete inferences depend on contingent empirical presuppositions of existence, uniqueness, persistence, and coreference that are not fixed by abstract logical competence, then whether a concrete inference instantiates a valid logical form is subject to environmental logical luck.
3. Therefore, whether a concrete inference instantiates a valid logical form is subject to environmental logical luck.
