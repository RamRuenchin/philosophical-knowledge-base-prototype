# Gertler 2015 - Internalism, Externalism, and Accessibilism

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Gertler-2015-Internalism Externalism and Accessibilism-In Sanford Goldberg Externalism Self Knowledge and Skepticism.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Gertler-2015-Internalism Externalism and Accessibilism-In Sanford Goldberg Externalism Self Knowledge and Skepticism|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

In this chapter, Brie Gertler addresses the debate between internalism and externalism regarding cognitive value—the aspect of thought content that figures in rational explanations of deliberation, inference, and behavior. Gertler argues that the traditional debate has been distorted by a conflation between mentalism (the thesis that content supervenes on factors internal to the thinker) and accessibilism (the thesis that content or its justification is accessible to reflective introspective awareness). Internalists have alleged that externalism cannot accommodate privileged introspective self-knowledge, while externalists have charged that internalism implausibly requires that thinkers have reflective explicatory access to their thoughts' truth conditions. Gertler argues that higher-order introspective and explicatory access is a red herring: externalism can accommodate privileged self-knowledge via content inheritance, while rational thought does not require reflective explicatory access, being instead a matter of first-order sensitivity among mental states. The genuine issue dividing internalism and externalism is whether external factors directly bear on rational relations among first-order thoughts. To resolve this question, Gertler formulates a qualified version of Frege's Differential Dubitability thesis—holding that for non-trivial, non-occurrent, non-a-priori contents, thoughts differ in cognitive value if and only if a rational thinker can differentially doubt them. She defends this thesis against Tyler Burge's alleged counterexamples involving mistaken criterial beliefs (such as subject A's religious theory of sofas), demonstrating that deference to communal norms is a first-order disposition that preserves differential dubitability rather than undermining it. Because cognitive values are individuated narrowly by first-order rational relations, Gertler concludes that thought contents individuated by cognitive value supervene on factors internal to the thinker, establishing a narrow, Fregean defense of content internalism.
Source: pp. 119–141.

## Main Summary

Feldman and Conee (2001) distinguish mentalism (justification supervenes on factors internal to the thinker) from accessibilism (justification is accessible to the thinker). In content debates, "content internalism" corresponds to mentalism: thought contents supervene on internal factors, while externalism denies this supervenience (p. 119). However, debates frequently target accessibilism: internalists allege externalism is incompatible with privileged introspective access, while externalists charge that internalism overestimates access by demanding reflective explicatory access to truth conditions (p. 119).

Focusing on higher-order access is misplaced, even regarding cognitive value—the content aspect explaining deliberation, inference, and rational behavior (pp. 119–120). Higher-order access cannot resolve the internalism-externalism dispute; instead, the debate hinges on whether external factors directly affect rational relations among first-order thoughts (p. 120). Shifting away from accessibilism neutralizes both traditional objections while supporting a Fregean, narrow individuation of cognitive value, ultimately vindicating content internalism (pp. 120–121).

### Part 1 Externalism and access

#### Externalism’s alleged incompatibility with introspective access

The initial incompatibility worry claims externalism cannot accommodate privileged introspective access to our thoughts (p. 121). Externalists readily defuse this via content inheritance (Burge 1988; Gibbons 1996; Wikforss 2008a): higher-order judgments ("I am thinking that p") inherit their content from the lower-order thoughts they embed or causally track, securing reliable, privileged self-knowledge without environmental investigation (p. 121).

A more nuanced worry (Goldberg 2003c) notes that knowing that one is thinking that p does not ensure knowing what one is thinking (p. 121). This targets the Explicatory Access thesis: no thought can rationally contribute by virtue of content unless the thinker can explicate its truth conditions and concept criteria purely through reflection (p. 122). This tension drives McKinsey reductios (introspecting water thoughts yields a priori knowledge of external environments) and slow-switching puzzles (switched thinkers introspectively detecting truth-conditional shifts) (pp. 122–123). Externalists like Burge resolve this not by abandoning externalism, but by rejecting Explicatory Access: Bert thinks rationally and knows he thinks he has arthritis despite false criterial beliefs about arthritis afflicting the thigh (pp. 123–124). Because Bert cannot correct this error by reflection alone, rational thought does not require reflective explicatory access (pp. 123–124).

#### Explicatory knowledge and rationality

A simple argument for Explicatory Access claims that rational inference (e.g. inferring q from "p or q" and "not-p") requires understanding what makes the transition rational, which demands grasp of truth-conditional relations (p. 124). Without explicatory knowledge, transitions would seem brute rather than rational (p. 124).

However, rational comprehension requires only first-order sensitivity, not higher-order explicatory access (pp. 124–125). Following Shoemaker (1994), rationality essentially consists in sensitivity to belief-desire contents, updating attitudes in light of experience and eliminating inconsistencies (p. 125). In the Rita case, planning a beach trip after a sunny forecast but abandoning it upon seeing dark clouds manifests rational sensitivity entirely at the first-order level (pp. 125–126). Rita need not entertain higher-order thoughts about her beliefs or explicate why dark clouds indicate rain; her belief functions rationally through its cognitive value (p. 126). Just as higher-order desires do not explain the emergence of genuine agency, higher-order thoughts do not explain the emergence of rationality (p. 126). First-order sensitivity suffices for rational thought, justifying the externalist rejection of Explicatory Access (pp. 126–127).

#### Explicatory access and “transparency”

Debates on the "transparency" of content (e.g. Boghossian 1994; Brown 2004a) conflate two distinct phenomena: first-order transparency (sameness or difference of content systematically affecting first-order deliberation and inference) and higher-order transparency (introspective ability to reflectively determine whether two thoughts share content) (pp. 127–128). Rationality requires first-order transparency, but higher-order transparency is orthogonal to cognitive value (p. 128). Externalists and internalists can affirm first-order rational sensitivity while remaining neutral on reflective transparency (p. 128).

#### Summing up: externalism and access

Externalism is compatible with privileged introspective tracking via content inheritance and reasonably rejects Explicatory Access by grounding rationality in first-order sensitivity (pp. 128–129). Comprehending thought does not require reflective explicatory access to truth conditions (p. 129). Accessibilism is therefore a red herring; the true dispute centers on whether external factors directly affect rational relations among first-order thoughts (p. 129).

### Part 2 Differential dubitability

The debate over cognitive value centers on whether external factors directly determine a thought's rational relations (p. 129). Frege's thesis holds that if a rational thinker can believe that p while doubting that q, the thoughts differ in cognitive value (pp. 129–130). This reflects first-order sensitivity without requiring explicatory access, explaining why doubting that Hesperus is Phosphorus while believing Hesperus is Hesperus establishes that cognitive value varies independently of external reference (p. 130).

In Loar's (1988) chat/cat case, Paul differentially doubts "chats have tails" and "cats have tails," establishing a difference in cognitive value despite identical reference (p. 130). Externalism easily accommodates this because internal differences (distinct etiologies) can suffice for content differences (pp. 130–131). The genuine challenge to externalism is not whether differential dubitability is sufficient for difference in cognitive value, but whether it is necessary (Wikforss 2006; Brown 2004a): externalism implies differences in content that are not discernible from the first-person perspective and cannot be reflected in rational relations of differential doubt (p. 131).

#### The differential dubitability thesis

Individuating cognitive value requires a standard fine-grained enough to capture differences to which rational thinkers are sensitive (sufficiency), yet coarse-grained enough to exclude distinctions beyond their sensitivity (necessity) (pp. 131–132). Excluding trivial cases of indubitable/unbelievable thoughts, occurrent thoughts, and a priori equivalences, Gertler restricts the scope to pairs (p, q) satisfying: (i) p and q are rationally dubitable and believable without indubitable/unbelievable conjuncts; (ii) neither concerns occurrent thoughts; and (iii) 'p iff q' is not knowable a priori (pp. 132–133).

The Differential Dubitability thesis states: for any pair (p, q) meeting conditions (i)–(iii), p and q differ in cognitive value iff a rational thinker can believe that p while doubting that q, or vice versa (p. 133). This thesis conflicts directly with externalism: actual Bert (believing "I have arthritis in my thigh") and counterfactual Bert (where "arthritis" includes non-rheumatoid ailments) have thoughts meeting (i)–(iii) that cannot be differentially doubted by a rational thinker, yet externalism insists they differ in cognitive value (pp. 133–134).

#### Burge’s proposed modification to the Differential Dubitability thesis

To reconcile externalism with Fregeanism, Burge (1986) proposes that doubt must be supportable by publicly recognized methods (p. 134). Extended to necessity, Burge's modified criterion holds that p and q differ in cognitive value iff adherence to social norms enables a thinker to believe that p while doubting that q (p. 134). This externalizes cognitive value: actual and counterfactual Bert's thoughts differ in cognitive value because public medical methods distinguish arthritis from other rheumatoid ailments (pp. 134–135).

Burge motivates this by arguing that mistaken criterial beliefs undermine differential dubitability (pp. 135–136). A thinker believing all chairs have legs might not differentially doubt "I am seated on a chair" and "I am seated on a piece of furniture with legs intended for sitting," even though ski-lift chairs demonstrate distinct truth conditions (pp. 135–136). Burge concludes that explicatory ignorance decouples differential dubitability from cognitive value (p. 136).

#### Defending the Differential Dubitability thesis

Mistaken criterial beliefs do not undermine the Differential Dubitability thesis, because Burge's objection conflates first-order differential doubt with higher-order explicatory access (p. 136). In Burge's case of subject A, who believes sofas are religious artifacts, A seems unable to differentially doubt (r) "The green item in the corner is a certain sort of religious artifact" and (s) "The green item in the corner is a sofa" (pp. 136–137). Because (r) and (s) differ in cognitive value but seem non-differentially dubitable for A, this is alleged to refute necessity (p. 137).

However, this challenge fails upon analyzing concept possession (pp. 137–138). Burge credits A with the concept sofa by stipulating that A is willing to submit his theory to empirical testing and relinquish it if disproven by communal evidential norms (p. 137). But this deferential disposition enables A to differentially doubt (r) and (s): A can believe (s) while doubting (r) by imagining his experiments disproved his theory (pp. 137–138). Conversely, if A dogmatically refused to relinquish his theory under any evidence, he would not possess the concept sofa at all (possessing instead an idiosyncratic concept with empty extension), rendering him incapable of entertaining (s) (p. 138). Deference is thus necessary for concept possession and sufficient for differential dubitability (p. 138). Cognitive value supervenes on internal dispositions without requiring higher-order explicatory access (pp. 138–139).

#### Deference and indexicality

Internalism accommodates deference by construing deferential concepts as indexed to external factors, mirroring two-dimensional semantics where narrow primary intensions determine cognitive value (pp. 139–140). Burge (1986, 2006) objects that ordinary thinkers do not conceptualize arthritis or sofas through complex meta-level indexical analyses and cannot tell by reflection how they depend on others (p. 140).

Gertler replies that Burge's objection presupposes accessibilism by assuming that indexical concepts must be reflectively recognized as such (pp. 140–141). But internalism is not accessibilism: an internalist can hold that concepts supervene on internal dispositions to apply terms and defer to authorities without requiring introspective awareness of that deferential role (p. 140). Burge presents a false dilemma: either cognitive value depends constitutively on external factors, or thinkers possess reflective access to their thoughts' explications (p. 141). The viable third option is that cognitive values supervene on internal dispositions without requiring explicatory access (p. 141). Because Burge's arguments undermine only Explicatory Access, Gertler's Fregean defense shows that thought contents individuated by cognitive value supervene on factors internal to the thinker (p. 141).

## Arguments

### Rita Case Refutation of Reflective Explicatory Access *(pp. 124–129)*

1. Possibly, an agent updates beliefs and plans rationally through first-order sensitivity to content relations without higher-order reflective explicatory access.
2. Necessarily: if an agent updates beliefs and plans rationally through first-order sensitivity to content relations without higher-order reflective explicatory access, then some agent who performs rational inference is not in possession of higher-order reflective explicatory access.
3. Possibly, some agent who performs rational inference is not in possession of higher-order reflective explicatory access.
4. Therefore, it is not the case that necessarily, every agent who performs rational inference is in possession of higher-order reflective explicatory access.

### Differential Dubitability Argument for Narrow Cognitive Value *(pp. 129–141)*

1. Possibly, two microphysical and experiential duplicates differ solely in their linguistic communities' extension of the word 'arthritis'.
2. If (possibly, two microphysical and experiential duplicates differ solely in their linguistic communities' extension of the word 'arthritis'), then cognitive value supervenes solely on narrow factors internal to the thinker.
3. Therefore, cognitive value supervenes solely on narrow factors internal to the thinker.
