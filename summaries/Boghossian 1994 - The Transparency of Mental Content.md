# Boghossian 1994 - The Transparency of Mental Content

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Boghossian-1994-The Transparency of Mental Content-Philosophical Perspectives.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Boghossian-1994-The Transparency of Mental Content-Philosophical Perspectives|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

Michael Dummett famously claimed that meaning is epistemically transparent—that a thinker who attaches meaning to two expressions can know a priori, via introspection alone, whether their meanings are identical. Boghossian argues that externalist theories of mental content (Putnam, Burge), which individuate content in part by environmental factors, violate epistemic transparency far more radically than commonly recognized. While Kripke's Pierre case demonstrates externalism's failure of the transparency of sameness (identical contents appearing introspectively distinct), Boghossian uses a novel 'one-world traveling' slow-switching thought experiment (Peter transported to Twin Earth) to show that externalism also violates the transparency of difference (distinct contents appearing introspectively identical in memory versus current thought). Boghossian argues that epistemic transparency is an indispensable presupposition of our ordinary practices of assessing rationality (conceived as the a priori disposition to conform to logic) and explaining action psychologically. Just as philosophers exclude referentially transparent de re beliefs from rationality assessments because their logical relations are not knowable a priori, externalist de dicto beliefs must face the same disqualification once transparency fails. Because attempts to absolve subjects of irrationality without transparency fail, Boghossian concludes there is an unresolved tension between externalist semantics and rationality-based psychological explanation.
Source: pp. 33–50 (no author-provided abstract; synthesized).

## Main Summary

### Introduction

Michael Dummett proclaimed that meaning possesses an undeniable epistemic transparency: anyone attaching meaning to two words must be in a position to know introspectively and a priori whether their meanings are identical (p. 33). Dummett treated this claim as too obvious to require argument, following Frege and Russell in making transparency central to semantic and mental content (pp. 33–34). Boghossian restricts his focus to mental content, examining the foundational role epistemic transparency plays in our ordinary conception of mind (p. 34). He sets out to establish that externalist theories of mental content violate transparency to an unappreciated degree, and that abandoning transparency undermines our ordinary practices of evaluating rationality and constructing psychological explanations (pp. 34–35).

### Externalism, Self-Knowledge and Transparency

The minimal moral of the Twin Earth thought experiments of Putnam and Burge is that either mental contents are not individuated individualistically, or they are not individuated by truth conditions (pp. 35–36). Externalism adopts the former disjunct, holding that mental contents are individuated in part by relations to the physical or social environment (p. 36). Although this appears to threaten introspective access to our thoughts, externalists frequently claim there is no conflict between externalism and self-knowledge (p. 36). Donald Davidson captures this compatibilist conviction succinctly: whatever is responsible for determining the content of a first-order thought is equally responsible for determining the content of the second-order thought that one has that thought (p. 36). Formulated in a language of thought, whenever a sentence $S$ enters an agent's belief box, syntactic copying ensures that the sentence "I believe that $S$" enters as well, reliably securing true second-order beliefs (p. 36).

Boghossian grants that this mechanism guarantees reliable second-order belief, but argues that it falls drastically short of genuine introspective self-knowledge (pp. 36–37). Knowing the content of two sentences individually does not ground the capacity to know a priori whether they share the same content or possess distinct contents (p. 37). Nor does it allow an agent to discern whether two distinct tokens of the same sentence type express identical or different contents (p. 37). In short, Davidsonian reliabilism fails to secure introspective discriminability—the ability to tell one's thoughts apart—and therefore leaves the epistemic transparency of mental content entirely ungrounded (p. 37).

### Externalism, Transparency and the Semantics of Travel

Epistemic transparency divides into two distinct principles:
1. *Transparency of sameness*: if two token thoughts possess identical contents, the thinker must be able to know a priori that they do (p. 37).
2. *Transparency of difference*: if two token thoughts possess distinct contents, the thinker must be able to know a priori that they do (p. 37).

Externalism straightforwardly violates the transparency of sameness (p. 37). Saul Kripke's Pierre demonstrates this for Millian singular contents: Pierre assents to "London is pretty" and "Londres is pretty" without realizing they express the same thought (p. 37). Brian Loar's case of Paul adapts this failure to non-Millian, referentially opaque, socially individuated de dicto thoughts: Paul learns "chats" from his French nanny and "cats" from his parents, sincerely believing "All chats have tails" and "All cats have tails" without having any a priori introspective access to the fact that both sentences express the very same belief (pp. 37–38).

More significantly, externalism also violates the transparency of difference (p. 38). To demonstrate this converse failure, Boghossian analyzes the semantics of "one-world traveling cases" (slow switching) (p. 38). Suppose Peter is unwittingly transported from Earth to Twin Earth and resides there indefinitely (p. 38). Tyler Burge accepts that after sufficient time in the twin environment, Peter's occurrent thoughts switch: current desires and beliefs about his immediate surroundings express concepts involving twater and twin-Pavarotti (pp. 38–39). Crucially, however, memory beliefs rooted in past Earthly perceptions do not switch (pp. 39–40). When Peter reminisces on Twin Earth about meeting Luciano Pavarotti swimming in Lake Taupo, New Zealand, his memory thoughts are causally sustained by past Earthly perceptions and retain their Earthly contents (water, Pavarotti, Lake Taupo) (pp. 39–40). Because Peter experiences no internal cognitive disruption, tokens of the syntactic type "water" in his memory thoughts mean water, whereas tokens of the same type in his occurrent thoughts mean twater (p. 40). These two tokens possess distinct semantic values while appearing introspectively indistinguishable, decisively refuting the transparency of difference (p. 40). Without epistemic transparency, our practices of assessing rationality and explaining behavior produce absurd results (pp. 40–41).

### The Apriority of Logical Properties and De Re Thoughts

A de re belief is stipulated as one individuated by the object it is about, expressible by an of-clause and subtending a Millian proposition (p. 41). Burge and Jerry Fodor both argue that de re beliefs do not enter into assessments of rationality or psychological explanations of behavior (pp. 41–42). Boghossian provides the underlying rationale: if Jane sees a wholesome-looking apple, she forms the de re belief that $\langle\text{apple}_1 \text{ is wholesome}\rangle$; when she later sees the same apple with its blemish exposed, she forms the de re belief that $\langle\text{apple}_1 \text{ is not wholesome}\rangle$ (p. 42). Her beliefs logically contradict each other, yet she cannot discover this contradiction a priori; she can learn it only empirically by discovering that both encounters involved the same physical apple (p. 42). In general, the logical properties (consistency, contradiction) of de re propositions are not knowable a priori (p. 42).

### The Apriority of Logical Properties and Norms of Good Reasoning

Good reasoning consists in the ability and disposition to conform one's thoughts to the principles of logic on an a priori basis (p. 43). An envatted brain whose sensory experiences seamlessly duplicate normal life remains a perfectly good reasoner despite accumulating widespread false empirical beliefs (p. 43). Minimal rationality requires avoiding obvious logical contradictions upon reflection (p. 43). If de re thoughts entered into assessments of rationality, Jane would be convicted of irrationality for harboring contradictory beliefs she cannot resolve introspectively (p. 43). Because Jane is intuitively blameless, philosophers adopt the conservative solution: de re thoughts are barred from assessments of rationality, preserving the logical ban on contradiction exclusively for de dicto beliefs (p. 43).

### The Apriority of Logical Properties and Psychological Explanation

Psychological explanation similarly depends on counterfactual-supporting generalizations that mirror introspectively obvious logical consequences of an agent's propositional attitudes (pp. 43–44). For example, if an agent occurrently believes $p$ and intends to act if $p$, they will normally form the intention to act (p. 44). However, perfectly rational agents fail to obey these generalizations when formulated over de re thoughts (p. 44). If Jack intends to call the FBI whenever he is within fifty feet of a spy, and believes de re of his disguised college dean next to him at the beach that he is a spy, Jack fails to call the FBI yet remains entirely rational (p. 44). The standard conservative solution is to exempt de re thoughts from psychological explanation, preserving action-explaining generalizations only for de dicto thoughts (p. 44). Thus, both rationality assessment and psychological explanation require that the logical properties of intentional states be knowable a priori (p. 44).

### Reference and Epistemic Transparency

The logical properties of referentially individuated thoughts fail to be knowable a priori precisely because referential transparency entails epistemic opacity (pp. 44–45). A thinker cannot tell a priori whether "Tully is bald" and "Cicero is bald" express the same proposition on a Millian construal, and hence cannot tell introspectively whether their conjunction involves a contradiction (p. 45). 

Violations of the two transparency principles produce asymmetric logical defects:
- Violations of the transparency of sameness (Pierre) prevent a thinker from knowing a priori that a proposition is a logical consequence of their beliefs; though simple inferences that look valid remain valid, not all valid inferences look valid (p. 45).
- Violations of the transparency of difference (Peter) cause invalid inferences to appear valid (p. 45). In Peter's slow-switching case, Peter combines a general Twearthly premise ("Whoever floats on water [twater], gets wet") with an Earthly memory belief ("Pavarotti once floated on water [water]") to deduce "Pavarotti once got wet" (pp. 45–46). The inference appears to Peter to be an elementary application of universal instantiation, yet it commits an unnoticed fallacy of equivocation (four terms), deriving a false conclusion from true premises (p. 46).

Consequently, Burge's distinction between de re thoughts (excluded from psychology) and de dicto thoughts (the basis of psychology) collapses: externalist de dicto thoughts are equally non-transparent, leaving externalists with no principled basis for admitting them into rational psychology (p. 46).

### The Apriority of Logical Properties and the Introduction of Sense

Boghossian demonstrates that epistemic transparency is the unacknowledged foundation of Frege's canonical argument for sense over mere reference (pp. 46–47). The standard motivation for referential opacity begins with Mary sincerely asserting "Ali was a champ" and "Clay was not a champ" (p. 47). Under Millianism, Mary simply holds contradictory beliefs; to conclude that Mary's belief reports are referentially opaque, one must presuppose that attributing contradictory beliefs is unacceptable unless the contradiction is accessible a priori to the agent (pp. 47–48). Because Mary cannot discover the contradiction through introspective reflection alone, beliefs involving proper names must be epistemically transparent, which in turn necessitates positing a non-referential semantic level—Fregean sense (p. 48).

### Kripke's Puzzling Pierre

Kripke's puzzle about Pierre relies on the very same tacit demand for epistemic transparency (pp. 48–49). When Pierre assents to "Londres is pretty" and "London is not pretty," Kripke insists that Pierre cannot be convicted of inconsistency because a rational logician is in principle capable of noticing and correcting contradictory beliefs (pp. 48–49). Boghossian argues that Kripke's impression of an intractable puzzle arises not from the nature of belief itself, but from simultaneously demanding epistemic transparency while presupposing a non-transparent semantic theory (p. 49).

### A Simple Solution?

Can rationality be refashioned to accommodate non-transparent content without yielding absurdities (pp. 49–50)? Boghossian considers an obvious proposal: absolve a thinker of irrationality for believing a contradiction provided that the contradictory character of their belief is inaccessible to mere a priori reflection (p. 50). He demonstrates that this proposal collapses into triviality: against the background of a non-transparent conception of content, virtually every contradictory belief is inaccessible to mere a priori reflection (p. 50). The proposed criterion would therefore excuse almost all instances of contradictory belief, gutting the norms of rationality entirely (p. 50).

### Conclusion

A pervasive and unresolved tension exists between our ordinary conceptions of rationality and psychological explanation (which presuppose that mental content is epistemically transparent) and dominant externalist theories of content (which entail that content is not transparent) (p. 50). Because neither jettisoning rationality nor refashioning it without transparency appears viable, philosophers must fundamentally reconsider either externalism or the role of transparency in mental life (p. 50).
