# Szubka 2000 - Meaning Rationalism, A Priori, and Transparency of Content

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Szubka-2000-Meaning rationalism a priori and transparency of content-Philosophical Psychology.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Szubka-2000-Meaning rationalism a priori and transparency of content-Philosophical Psychology|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

Most current theories of meaning and mental content accept externalism. One of its forceful exponents is Ruth Garrett Millikan. She argues that externalism leads to the abandonment of “the last myth of the given”, that is, of the idea that identity of meaning and mental content is somehow unproblematically given to us, and that we can easily recognize the sameness of meaning and mental content. If one refuses such a “mythical” giveness or meaning rationalism, one has to admit that there is no logical possibility known a priori. The paper tries to show that even if one abandons meaning rationalism one can still hold that there are logical possibilities known a priori. The claim is defended by arguing that a priori knowledge is not completely independent from experience and does not demand the absolute transparency of meaning from the first-person point of view. A priori knowledge requires only a priori justification, that is, such a justification that is based merely on relations between meanings or contents.
Source: p. 491 (PDF p. 2).

## Main Summary

### Introduction

Externalism is widely accepted in contemporary theories of meaning and mental content, holding that meaning and content are individuated not merely by internal or subjective factors, but primarily by external environmental relations of which a thinker may be unaware (citing Putnam and Burge). While Putnam and Burge defended restricted applications of externalism, other thinkers generalized it into a radical or global doctrine. Ruth Garrett Millikan is a prominent exponent of radical externalism, arguing in *White Queen Psychology and Other Essays for Alice* (1993) that externalism requires abandoning “the last myth of the given”—the assumption that the identity and difference of meanings and mental contents are unproblematically given to us and recognized a priori. Millikan claims this rejection yields three major consequences: first, that no logical possibility is knowable a priori; second, that because rationality requires reidentifying thought contents, rationality is environmentally dependent rather than an a priori capacity of the mind; and third, that intentional rational psychology must be transformed into an ecological and biological science. Szubka bounds his inquiry by focusing on the first consequence, arguing that even if meaning rationalism is rejected, there remains a legitimate sense in which logical possibility is known a priori. He further distinguishes strong from weak transparency of content, defending the weak version as an indispensable first-person requirement. Source: pp. 491–492 (PDF pp. 2–3).

### Meaning rationalism attacked

Millikan’s primary target is “meaning rationalism,” an epistemological conception of meaning transparency consisting of three theses: (1) the epistemic givenness of meaning identity and difference (a rational person can recognize a priori whether any two thoughts express the same meaning); (2) the epistemic givenness of univocity (a rational person can recognize a priori whether a thought is ambiguous); and (3) the epistemic givenness of meaningfulness (a rational person can discern a priori whether an expression or thought has meaning or is empty). Because thesis (2) follows from (1), refuting (1) and (3) defeats the doctrine. Millikan clarifies that “a rational person” must designate a mechanically or psychologically rational subject (one whose cognitive faculties are healthy, intact, and functioning) rather than a semantically rational subject (one whose thought transitions necessarily conform to logical norms), since defining rationality semantically would render meaning rationalism trivially true and question-begging. Millikan then distinguishes three identity criteria for terms and propositions: co-extensionality, content rationalism (identity via extension and compositional structure), and mode rationalism (identity via extension, structure, and Fregean mode of presentation). Co-extensionality is dismissed as implausibly forcing anyone who believes a truth to believe all logically equivalent truths. Against content rationalists, Millikan argues that the primitive elements of thought content cannot be epistemically given without discredited direct-apprehension models (such as Platonic forms, Ockhamist direct apprehension, or Russellian acquaintance with sense data, which succumb to Wittgensteinian and Sellarsian critiques); given causal-historical externalism, thinkers cannot reidentify simple referents a priori. Consequently, logical possibility cannot be known a priori on a content-based view, because one cannot identify content-contradictory thoughts a priori under negation. Against mode rationalists (who invoke Frege’s view that a rational thinker cannot affirm and deny the same content under the same mode of presentation), Millikan argues that this makes logical possibilities an epistemically heterogeneous class (some knowable a priori, others not), reduces logical possibilities to mere epistemic states rather than objective sets of possible worlds, and ultimately fails because modes of presentation are either non-semantic psychological roles or not given a priori. Millikan concludes that meaning rationalism and a priori logical possibility must be abandoned together. Source: pp. 492–495 (PDF pp. 3–6).

### A priori knowledge defended

Szubka defends a priori knowledge against Millikan’s conclusions on several fronts. First, he points out that if Millikan targets the unrestricted thesis that *all* logical possibilities and truths can be established a priori via deductive proof, she attacks a straw man already refuted by metalogical limitative results, such as the undecidability of first-order predicate logic. If she targets the modest view that *some* logical possibilities are knowable a priori, the epistemic heterogeneity of logical possibilities is entirely unproblematic. Second, Szubka provides a principled defense by refining what it means for knowledge to be a priori. Drawing on Philip Kitcher (1980), he observes that a priori knowledge is independent of experience not in the sense that experience plays no role in acquiring concepts, but in the sense that any experience sufficient to acquire the concepts involved enables attaining the knowledge; the warranting process itself does not depend on experience. Drawing on Tyler Burge (1993), Szubka emphasizes that a justification or entitlement is a priori if its justificational force is not constituted or enhanced by reference to or reliance on the specifics of sensory experiences, even though perceptual experience is typically required for concept acquisition and understanding. Thus, one can accept Millikan’s externalism and third-person concept acquisition while maintaining that the justification of logical truths is a priori, depending strictly on relations among meanings or contents. When critics claim that there are no meaning-based justifications, they must treat concepts as unstructured and isolated. Reflection on concept possession shows that possessing a concept (such as *square*) involves dispositions to accept constitutive truths (e.g., that a square is a four-sided figure), allowing a priori justification by unpacking concept possession conditions. Szubka addresses the objection that this conflates constitutive conditions with empirical knowledge (such as learning about squares in school) by proposing that thought experiments identify statements that hold stably across worlds where the concept is possessed. Against the objection that unpacking may be fallible and thus fail traditional rationalist criteria (such as Cartesian certainty, indubitability, and incorrigibility), Szubka argues that demanding infallibility unfairly rigs the definition of aprioricity to guarantee its impossibility; fallible unpacking of concepts preserves genuine a priori justification. Finally, he notes that radical externalism’s alternative—treating logic as an inductive empirical science—conflicts with developmental psychological evidence showing early sensitivity to numerical and logical relations in infants (Wynn, McGinn), and that appealing to evolutionary innate mechanisms still relies on an overly restrictive definition of the a priori. Source: pp. 495–500 (PDF pp. 6–11).

### Transparency of content moderated

Szubka examines whether Millikan’s attack successfully refutes transparency of content more broadly. He contrasts Millikan’s three-part “meaning rationalism” with Michael Dummett’s classical formulation of meaning transparency: someone who attaches a meaning to two words must know (a priori) whether their meanings are the same. Millikan adds a third condition—the epistemic givenness of meaningfulness—which on externalist premises would implausibly grant a priori knowledge of external reality and provide an easy refutation of skepticism. Dummett’s requirement does not include this inflated third condition. Furthermore, Szubka identifies a crucial distinction between diachronic and synchronic transparency. Millikan attacks diachronic transparency: the demand that a thinker can reidentify thoughts and referents across time a priori (her strong construal of Russell’s Principle). However, standard transparency requirements are synchronic and modest: a thinker who attaches a meaning to two expressions at a particular time must know a priori, at that time, whether those meanings are identical or distinct. Szubka contends that this modest synchronic transparency cannot be dismantled by Millikan’s anti-rationalist arguments. Millikan’s insistence to the contrary stems from her epistemological axiom asserting the strict priority of the third-person point of view over the first-person point of view. Szubka acknowledges that subjects are natural entities in the empirical world, but warns that claiming unconstrained third-person superiority amounts to adopting an unattainable “God’s eye view.” A moderated, first-person synchronic transparency of content remains viable and necessary. Source: pp. 500–502 (PDF pp. 11–13).

## Arguments

### Szubka Warrant-Based A Priori Without Strong Meaning Rationalism *(pp. 495–502)*

1. It is not the case that every thinker has an infallible a priori grasp of concept identity, difference, univocity, and non-emptiness purely from within.
2. A priori knowledge denotes fallible knowledge whose epistemic warrant does not derive justificational force from sensory experience.
3. If both (it is not the case that every thinker has an infallible a priori grasp of concept identity, difference, univocity, and non-emptiness purely from within) and (a priori knowledge denotes fallible knowledge whose epistemic warrant does not derive justificational force from sensory experience), then logical possibilities are knowable through fallible knowledge whose epistemic warrant does not derive justificational force from sensory experience.
4. Both (it is not the case that every thinker has an infallible a priori grasp of concept identity, difference, univocity, and non-emptiness purely from within) and (a priori knowledge denotes fallible knowledge whose epistemic warrant does not derive justificational force from sensory experience).
5. Therefore, logical possibilities are knowable through fallible knowledge whose epistemic warrant does not derive justificational force from sensory experience.
