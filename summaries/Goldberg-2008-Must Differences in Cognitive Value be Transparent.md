# Goldberg 2008 - Must Differences in Cognitive Value be Transparent

**Raw source:** [[raw_sources/Goldberg-2008-Must Differences in Cognitive Value be Transparent-Erkenntnis.pdf|PDF]] · **Converted Markdown:** [[converted_markdown/Goldberg-2008-Must Differences in Cognitive Value be Transparent|Converted Markdown]]

## Abstract

Frege’s ‘differential dubitability’ test is a test for differences in cognitive value: if one can rationally believe that p while simultaneously doubting that q, then the contents p and q amount to different ‘cognitive values’. If subject S is rational, does her simultaneous adoption of different attitudes towards p and q require that the difference between p and q (as cognitive values) be transparent to her? It is natural to think so. But I argue that, if attitude anti-individualism is true, then rational differential dubitability does not presuppose that differences in cognitive value are transparent. The significance of this argument lies in what it tells us, both about the notion of cognitive value and its relation to the differential dubitability test, but also about the prospects for a Burge-type position which aims to combine attitude anti-individualism with a (qualified) reliance on the differential dubitability test.
Source: p. 1.

## Main Summary

### 1

Goldberg formulates Frege’s Test (FT): if someone can rationally believe one content while simultaneously doubting another, the two contents differ. A tempting constraint says that such rational differential doubt requires transparency of the content difference. Goldberg formulates this as (Tr-D): whenever a rational subject simultaneously entertains two distinct contents, reflection enables her to tell that their contents differ. FT does not itself entail Tr-D, however. A theorist can accept FT while denying that rationally different attitudes require reflective discrimination of the contents. The challenge is to explain how the attitudes can remain rational when the subject cannot tell the contents apart as contents. This challenge is especially pressing for Burgean anti-individualism, which individuates at least some attitudes partly by environmental or social factors and thereby allows content differences that are not reflectively transparent to the thinker. Goldberg aims to show how a Fregean anti-individualist can meet the challenge. Source: pp. 1-3.

### 2

Goldberg’s central case concerns Schmidt. At an earlier time on Earth she thinks, “Water was Granny’s favorite drink,” and after an unnoticed slow switch to Twin Earth she later thinks the same words again. Having come to suspect a switch, she continues to believe the earlier thought but doubts the later one. Anti-individualism assigns the two token thoughts different contents because the relevant natural-kind concepts are environmentally individuated: the earlier concept is WATER and the later one TWATER. On the “supplement” view of switching, the later acquisition does not replace the earlier concept, so Schmidt can recall and simultaneously entertain both thoughts. Yet the content difference is not introspectively discriminable to her, as standard slow-switching discussions assume. Source: pp. 3-6.

The apparent difficulty is that Schmidt may seem irrational: how could she believe one thought and doubt another if, for all she can tell, their contents are the same? Goldberg answers by distinguishing identifying two thoughts from discriminating their contents. Sid can identify identical twins separately through perceptual and memorial demonstratives—“the twin before me” and “the twin I saw earlier”—without possessing a reliable ability to tell the twins apart. Likewise, Schmidt can fix which thought she believes and which she doubts by demonstrative or descriptive routes tied to different episodes, even though she cannot distinguish the thoughts by content. Rational differential doubt requires an introspective way to differentiate the thought believed from the thought doubted; it does not require an ability to differentiate their contents as such. Source: pp. 6-8.

Goldberg rejects the suggestion that Schmidt really doubts only metacognitive propositions about her earlier and later thought tokens. Given the supplement view and Schmidt’s avowed recollection, that interpretation needs independent support; it cannot simply assume that differential doubt toward indiscriminable contents would be irrational. The parallel with Sid shows how reference-fixing demonstratives can determine which first-order content is entertained without becoming part of that content. The fact that Schmidt uses the same word-form “water” twice is consequently misleading rather than evidence of irrationality, because anti-individualism allows the word-form to correspond to two concepts. Source: pp. 8-9.

A higher-order version of the objection fares no better. Even if anti-individualism permits some nontransparent differences among Schmidt’s higher-order thoughts, it does not follow that she cannot distinguish the particular higher-order thought that picks out the earlier content from the one that picks out the later content. The demonstrative resources already identified can distinguish those particular higher-order thoughts. Goldberg concludes that, given anti-individualism and the supplement view, Schmidt rationally differentially doubts distinct contents without transparently discerning their content difference. The case therefore separates rational differential dubitability from transparency, although its unusual background motivates examining less idiosyncratic variants. Source: pp. 9-10.

### 3

Goldberg generalizes the result through two contrasting variants. McSorley falsely believes she was switched and thus initially appears to differentially doubt contents anti-individualism treats as identical. Jones really was switched but never considers that possibility and initially treats contents anti-individualism treats as distinct as though they were identical. Goldberg argues that a qualified application of FT can accommodate both perspectives. Source: p. 10.

#### 3.1

McSorley’s case creates a choice: either multiply contents in response to her apparent differential doubt, or preserve the anti-individualist verdict that only one content is involved by denying that the apparent doubt satisfies FT’s rational antecedent. Goldberg pursues the second option because treating every such case as a content difference risks individuating contents too finely. His independent motivation is semantic deference. Just as Bert’s misuse of “arthritis” does not determine a private THARTHRITIS concept when his disposition is to defer to linguistic experts, McSorley’s initial doubt does not settle her ultimate epistemic perspective. Her deferential dispositions can override the preliminary disposition elicited by the test. Source: pp. 10-13.

This does not discard FT’s result, Goldberg argues, because FT applies only where a subject *can rationally* believe one content while doubting another. The antecedent is both modal and normative. If the two token thoughts have one content—as standard anti-individualism independently says in McSorley’s unchanged environment—then the apparent differential doubt is either impossible or irrational. The appeal to content identity is not question-begging within Goldberg’s defensive project, whose question is whether someone already committed to anti-individualism can consistently retain FT. Semantic deference supplies a motivated account of why McSorley’s first response is not her final rational perspective. Source: pp. 13-15.

#### 3.2

Jones presents the converse illusion. Although she was slow-switched and therefore possesses distinct WATER and TWATER thoughts, she has no initial inclination to doubt them differentially. FT is nevertheless modal: the question is whether she *can* rationally do so. Goldberg compares Ralph, who initially accepts both “Brianna is an attorney” and “Brianna is a lawyer” but can rationally question their equivalence after directed reflection. Jones too can reflect on whether superficially identical watery substances might be distinct natural kinds and thereby acquire rational differential doubt. The reflection is more philosophical, but that difference does not defeat the relevant possibility. Source: pp. 15-16.

The worry that directed reflection could manufacture differential doubt in every case is answered by the McSorley analysis. Where the supposed difference conflicts with the subject’s deferential semantic commitments and there is only one governing standard, the initial doubt can be rejected as not ultimately rational. Thus the method does not indiscriminately multiply contents. Goldberg concedes that applying FT becomes more complicated in rare cases involving actual or suspected switching, but argues that this qualification does not remove its general probative value. Source: pp. 16-17.

### 4

Goldberg next explains why the qualified test still captures cognitive value, understood as the subject’s epistemic perspective or ways of conceiving things. FT tracks distinctions a subject is rationally disposed to make, and these ordinarily track differences among her conceptions. Anti-individualism complicates the interpretation because a subject’s conceptions can depend on linguistic facts inaccessible to her. Jones may initially take two concepts to be one; McSorley may initially take one concept to be two. Preliminary differential-doubt dispositions are therefore reliable but defeasible evidence of cognitive value. Source: pp. 17-18.

The first controversial consequence is that a subject’s first-blush judgments do not invariably determine her cognitive values. Goldberg answers that cognitive value is a theoretical notion connected not only to differential doubt but also to how the agent represents the world and to what determines the truth of her representations. In McSorley’s unchanged linguistic environment, her two recollected “water” thoughts are governed by the same deferential standards and concern the same kind in the same way. Treating her momentary doubt as decisive would distort those other theoretical connections. Her semantic deference makes overriding the preliminary response analogous to treating Bert’s thigh application as a misuse of ARTHRITIS rather than the correct use of another concept. Source: pp. 18-20.

The second controversial consequence is that public linguistic standards help constitute an individual’s epistemic perspective. Goldberg defends this through testimony. Hearers routinely acquire the specific belief or knowledge communicated by speakers despite knowing little about those speakers’ idiosyncratic interpretive dispositions. This efficiency would be mysterious if speaker and hearer did not inherit shared conceptions from their public language. Without such common standards, communication would become an unstable coordination problem and could not reliably transmit the particular proposition asserted. Since testimony is central to acquiring and sustaining knowledge, semantic deference is likewise central to the subject’s own cognitive perspective. Appealing to public standards therefore does not open a gap between her perspective and “her own” conceptions; it explains how those conceptions are acquired, shared, and governed. Source: pp. 20-22.

### 5

Goldberg concludes that FT remains useful but its modal-normative antecedent must be interpreted carefully. Rational differential dubitability does not require transparent access to content differences. Jones illustrates a false negative at the level of initial appearance: rational differential doubt is possible although absent at first. McSorley illustrates a false positive: apparent rational differential doubt is defeated by the subject’s deeper deferential commitments. Anti-individualism explains these “illusions of rational differential dubitability” systematically and can therefore combine qualified reliance on FT with denial of Tr-D. Source: p. 22.
